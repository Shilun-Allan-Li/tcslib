## 1.1 Background – Secure Computation

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p003-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=3; block=3; confidence=0.99 -->
Multiparty computation seeks to evaluate a function of parties’ private inputs while providing privacy, correctness, input independence, and fairness or guaranteed output delivery. The ideal/real simulation paradigm defines these goals by comparing protocol execution with an ideal trusted-party computation. Adversary models vary by behavior (semi-honest or malicious), computational power (bounded or unbounded), and corruption timing (static or adaptive).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p003-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=3; block=4; confidence=0.98 -->
Prior results established computational feasibility, followed by information-theoretic feasibility for arbitrary functionalities with private channels when fewer than one third of parties are corrupted; one such result gave perfect security and another statistical security. Adding broadcast enabled statistical security with an honest majority.

## 1.2 The BGW Protocol

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p003-b006"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=3; block=6; confidence=0.99 -->
BGW showed that every functionality admits perfect security against semi-honest adversaries corrupting a minority and malicious adversaries corrupting fewer than one third. The introduction emphasizes the influence of this result and the zero-cheating-probability guarantee of perfect security.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p003-b007"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=3; block=7; confidence=0.93 -->
The protocol computes a function $f$ mapping $n$ inputs to $n$ outputs by securely emulating an arithmetic circuit. The description continues beyond the supplied page.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p004-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=4; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
The parties share circuit inputs, emulate gates on shares, and reconstruct output-wire secrets. Addition can be handled locally; multiplication requires interaction to restore the sharing degree.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p004-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=4; block=2; confidence=0.99 -->
For a finite field \(F\) with \(|F|>n\), distinct nonzero points \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n\), secret \(s\in F\), and threshold \(t\), choose uniformly a degree-\(t\) polynomial \(p\in F[x]\) with \(p(0)=s\); party \(P_i\) receives \(p(\alpha_i)\). Any \(t+1\) shares determine \(p\) by interpolation and recover \(s=p(0)\); any at most \(t\) shares reveal no information about \(s\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p004-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=4; block=3; confidence=0.98 -->
Each party first shares its input. The semi-honest protocol uses Shamir sharing for \(t<n/2\); the malicious protocol uses VSS for \(t<n/3\), to detect or prevent cheating.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p004-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=4; block=4; confidence=0.99 -->
Given input sharings \(p(\alpha_i)\) and \(q(\alpha_i)\), each party computes \(r(\alpha_i)=p(\alpha_i)+q(\alpha_i)\). Since \(r(x)=p(x)+q(x)\) has degree at most \(t\) and \(r(0)=p(0)+q(0)\), these are valid shares of the sum.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p004-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=4; block=5; confidence=0.98 -->
Computing \(r(\alpha_i)=p(\alpha_i)q(\alpha_i)\) gives shares of a polynomial with constant term \(p(0)q(0)\), but degree \(2t\); the protocol therefore reduces the degree back to \(t\). In the semi-honest case this is possible for \(t<n/2\), which ensures \(2t<n\) so the degree-\(2t\) polynomial is determined by the parties' \(n\) evaluations. Malicious degree reduction works for \(t<n/3\). The Reed–Solomon view gives intuition: a degree-\(t\) polynomial code corrects up to \((n-t-1)/2\) errors, and \(t<n/3\) implies \(n\ge 3t+1\), hence \((n-t-1)/2\ge t\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p005-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=5; block=1; confidence=0.98 -->
The malicious threshold \(t<n/3\) is also necessary in general because Byzantine agreement has the same bound. With broadcast, information-theoretic secure computation is possible for \(t<n/2\).

## 2 Preliminaries and Definitions

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p007-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=7; block=3; confidence=0.99 -->
The definitions use a stand-alone synchronous execution with perfect private channels and broadcast, static corruption of at most $t$ parties, and perfect security. Missing messages are detectable by round; adaptive corruption and composition are deferred to Section 8.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p007-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=7; block=4; confidence=0.95 -->
For a set $A$, $a\in_R A$ means uniform sampling. There are $n$ parties and at most $t$ corruptions. A possibly probabilistic functionality is $f:({0,1}^*)^n\to({0,1}^*)^n$, with $f_i(\vec x)$ its $i$th output. For corrupted indices $I=\{i_1,\ldots,i_\ell\}\subseteq[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}$, $|I|\le t$, define $\vec x_I=(x_{i_1},\ldots,x_{i_\ell})$ and $f_I(\vec x)=(f_{i_1}(\vec x),\ldots,f_{i_\ell}(\vec x))$. The notation $\{a_i\}_{i=1}^n$ may be shortened to $\{a_i\}$; indexed shares on $I$ may be written $\{f(\alpha_i)\}_{i\in I}$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p007-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=7; block=5; confidence=0.99 -->
Security against a semi-honest adversary controlling $t$ parties is called $t$-privacy; against a malicious adversary controlling $t$ parties it is called $t$-security. Both terms mean perfect security here.

## 2.1 Perfect Security in the Presence of Semi-Honest Adversaries

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p008-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=8; block=2; confidence=0.97 -->
A protocol is $t$-private when the view of up to $t$ corrupted parties can be simulated from their inputs and outputs. Party $P_i$'s view is $\operatorname{view}_i^\pi(\vec x)=(x_i,r_i;m_{i1},\ldots,m_{ik})$, comprising its input, random coins, and received messages. For $I=\{i_1,\ldots,i_\ell\}$, $\operatorname{view}_I^\pi(\vec x)$ is the tuple of these views; $\operatorname{output}^\pi(\vec x)$ is the parties' output, computable from each party's view.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p008-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=8; block=3; confidence=0.99 -->
**Definition 2.1.** Let $f:({0,1}^*)^n\to({0,1}^*)^n$ be deterministic and $\pi$ a protocol. The protocol is $t$-private for $f$ if for every $\vec x$ with $|x_1|=\cdots=|x_n|$, correctness holds:
\[
\operatorname{output}^\pi(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=f(x_1,\ldots,x_n).\tag{2.1}
\]
Also, there is a probabilistic polynomial-time algorithm $S$ such that for every $I\subseteq[n]$, $|I|\le t$, and every such $\vec x$,
\[
S(I,\vec x_I,f_I(\vec x))\equiv \operatorname{view}_I^\pi(\vec x).\tag{2.2}
\]
Here $\equiv$ denotes equality of distributions.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p008-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=8; block=4; confidence=0.99 -->
For deterministic $f$, correctness and view simulation are separate conditions; for probabilistic $f$, correctness and privacy are expressed jointly by the simulator/output distribution.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p008-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=8; block=5; confidence=0.99 -->
**Definition 2.2.** Let $f:({0,1}^*)^n\to({0,1}^*)^n$ be probabilistic and $\pi$ a protocol. It is $t$-private for $f$ if some probabilistic polynomial-time $S$ satisfies, for every $I\subseteq[n]$ with $|I|\le t$ and every equal-length input tuple $\vec x$,
\[
\bigl(S(I,\vec x_I,f_I(\vec x)),f(\vec x)\bigr)\equiv\bigl(\operatorname{view}_I^\pi(\vec x),\operatorname{output}^\pi(\vec x)\bigr).\tag{2.3}
\]

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p008-b006"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=8; block=6; confidence=0.99 -->
When $f$ is deterministic, (2.1) and (2.2) imply the joint distributional condition (2.3), since $f(\vec x)$ has a single value.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p009-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=9; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
The BGW security proof is given for deterministic functionalities using Definition 2.1; the paper cites a reduction from general probabilistic functionalities to deterministic ones.

## 2.2 Perfect Security in the Presence of Malicious Adversaries

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p009-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=9; block=3; confidence=0.99 -->
A malicious adversary may use any strategy and need not be efficient. Security compares a real protocol execution with an ideal execution in which inputs are sent to a trusted functionality.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p009-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=9; block=4; confidence=0.96 -->
**Real model.** Parties execute $\pi$ over synchronous private point-to-point channels and an authenticated broadcast channel. The adversary cannot read or alter private messages or tamper with authenticated broadcasts, but is rushing: within each round it sees honest parties' messages before choosing corrupted parties' messages. For an $n$-party protocol, arbitrary adversary machine $A$ with auxiliary input $z$, and corrupted set $I\subseteq[n]$, $\operatorname{REAL}_{\pi,A(z),I}(\vec x)$ denotes the random variable of $A$'s view together with honest parties' outputs in the execution on inputs $\vec x$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p009-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=9; block=5; confidence=0.97 -->
**Ideal model.** The simulator $S$, with auxiliary input $z$, sees inputs of parties in $I$ and may replace each $x_i$ by $x'_i$ subject to $|x'_i|=|x_i|$. The trusted party receives the resulting input tuple $(x'_1,\ldots,x'_n)$ and computes $(y_1,\ldots,y_n)=f(x'_1,\ldots,x'_n)$, sending $y_j$ to each $P_j$. Honest parties output $y_j$; corrupted parties output $\bot$; $S$ outputs an arbitrary function of its view.

**Claim 3.2.** Let α₁,…,αₙ be distinct nonzero elements of F, let s,s′∈F, and let I⊂[n] have size ℓ≤t. For every y⃗∈F^ℓ, if f and g are independently uniform in P_{s,t} and P_{s′,t}, respectively, then Pr[y⃗=(f(αᵢ))_{i∈I}]=Pr[y⃗=(g(αᵢ))_{i∈I}]=1/|F|^ℓ.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p013-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=13; block=3; confidence=0.97 -->
**Proof.** First take |I|=t. Given y⃗∈F^t, there is a unique degree-at-most-t polynomial through (0,s) and the points (αᵢ,yᵢ), i∈I. Thus exactly one member of P_{s,t} yields y⃗, giving probability 1/|P_{s,t}|=1/|F|^t. The same holds with s′, since both polynomial sets have size |F|^t. For ℓ<t, extend I to J⊂[n] of size t; sum over all y⃗′∈F^{t−ℓ} on J\\I. Each extension has probability 1/|F|^t, and there are |F|^{t−ℓ} extensions, so the marginal probability is 1/|F|^ℓ for either secret.

## 4.2 Private Computation in the $F_{\mathrm{mult}}$-Hybrid Model

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p017-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=17; block=3; confidence=0.99 -->
The section considers deterministic $f$ computed by an arithmetic circuit over a known finite field $F$, with one input per party, and assumes $|F|>n$. The protocol is claimed to be $t$-private in the hybrid model for every $t<n$, although implementing $F_{\mathrm{mult}}$ will require $t<n/2$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p017-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=17; block=4; confidence=0.99 -->
**Theorem 4.2.** Let $F$ be a finite field, $f:F^n\to F^n$, and $t<n$. Protocol 4.1 $t$-privately computes $f$ in the $F_{\mathrm{mult}}$-hybrid model against a static semi-honest adversary.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p017-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=17; block=5; confidence=0.97 -->
**Proof.** Correctness follows because the circuit computes $f$, so $\mathrm{output}_\pi(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$. For privacy, the proof will construct a simulator and inductively compare adversarial partial views at each stage. Before output reconstruction, the observed values are random shares; by Claim 3.2, any $t$ shares have the same distribution for any two possible secrets, so simulated shares may be generated from arbitrary values. At the output stage, interpolation lets the simulator choose a degree-$t$ polynomial through the corrupted parties' shares with constant term equal to the actual output. The proof then begins defining notation for the simulator.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p018-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=18; block=1; confidence=1.00 -->
**Protocol 4.1 (t-Private Computation in the $F_{\mathrm{mult}}$-Hybrid Model).**

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p018-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=18; block=2; confidence=0.99 -->
Each party $P_i$ has input $x_i\in F$. All parties know a circuit $C$ over $F$ computing $f:F^n\to F^n$, the field description, and distinct nonzero evaluation points $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p018-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=18; block=3; confidence=0.99 -->
1. **Input sharing:** Each $P_i$ samples $q_i$ uniformly from $P_{x_i,t}$ and sends $q_i(\alpha_j)$ to each $P_j$; each party records the shares it receives. 2. **Circuit emulation:** For gates in a fixed topological order, parties locally add input shares for addition gates, locally multiply shares by $c$ for scalar-multiplication gates, and send pairs of input shares to $F_{\mathrm{mult}}$ for multiplication gates, receiving the output share. 3. **Output reconstruction:** For output wires $o_1,\ldots,o_n$, parties send the shares of wire $o_k$ to recipient $P_k$. That party reconstructs the polynomial from the shares (noting that $t+1$ suffice) and outputs its value at zero.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p018-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=18; block=4; confidence=0.98 -->
The view is augmented with $\sigma_i^k$, the shares held by $P_i$ after gate $G_k$, and written $\mathrm{view}_\pi^i(\vec{x})=(x_i,r_i;m_i^1,\sigma_i^1,\ldots,m_i^\ell,\sigma_i^\ell)$. Since these shares are computable from the input, random tape, and received messages, this augmented view is equivalent to the original. The simulator sends arbitrary-value random shares until output reconstruction, then chooses shares whose reconstruction gives the actual output; the page ends at the simulator heading.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p019-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=19; block=1; confidence=0.95 -->
**Simulation.** The simulator chooses \(\widehat I\supseteq I\) of cardinality exactly \(t\), with \(\widehat I\subseteq[n]\). For each \(i\in I\), it chooses a uniformly distributed random tape for \(P_i\), which together with input \(x_i\) determines that party’s degree-\(t\) polynomial \(q_i(x)\). For each \(j\notin I\), it chooses a random degree-\(t\) polynomial \(q'_j(x)\in_R\mathcal P^{0,t}\). It constructs the view of each corrupted party \(P_i\) from \(\{q_j(\alpha_i)\}_{j\in[n],\,i\in\widehat I}\). For each addition gate, it computes the output shares by adding the input shares; for each multiplication-by-constant gate, it multiplies the input shares by the constant. For each multiplication gate, it chooses a degree-\(t\) polynomial uniformly at random from \(\mathcal P^{0,t}\), independently of the input shares, and uses its evaluations as the output shares for parties in \(\widehat I\). For each output wire \(o_k\), it interpolates the unique degree-\(t\) polynomial \(q'_k(x)\) satisfying \(q'_k(0)=y_k\) and \(q'_k(\alpha_i)=\beta_k^i\) for every \(i\in\widehat I\), then adds \(\{q'_k(\alpha_1),\ldots,q'_k(\alpha_n)\}\) to \(P_k\)’s view.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p020-b001"></a>

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p020-b002"></a>

**Claim 4.3.** For every $\vec x\in F^n$ and $I\subseteq[n]$ with $|I|\le t$, the real and simulated partial views have identical distributions: $\{\widehat{\mathrm{view}}^\pi_I(\vec x)\}\equiv\{\widetilde S(I,\vec x_I,f_I(\vec x))\}$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p020-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=20; block=3; confidence=0.97 -->
**Proof.** Define a hybrid algorithm $H$ taking $n-|I|+\ell$ sets of shares indexed by corrupted parties: $n-|I|$ sets for honest-party input shares and $\ell$ sets for multiplication-gate shares. It inserts these into the partial view and otherwise follows $S$. If all supplied shares come from random polynomials with constant term $0$, the result is the simulator’s partial view. If they come from polynomials with constants determined by the honest inputs and circuit wire values, the result is the real partial view. Claim 3.2 gives identical distributions for these share sets in the two cases, hence the partial views are identically distributed.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p020-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=20; block=4; confidence=0.98 -->
To compare the remaining stage, the proof notes that the shares on $\hat I$ are identically distributed in the real execution and simulation by Claim 3.2, then defines deterministic processes for each execution to construct output polynomials.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p021-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=21; block=1; confidence=0.96 -->
Given input-wire values, multiplication-gate output values, and the $t$ shares on $\hat I$ for each input or multiplication-output wire, the real process interpolates the degree-$t$ polynomials using those shares and their constants. Since each output wire is a linear combination of input wires and multiplication-gate outputs, it forms each corrupted output polynomial by the same linear combination.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p021-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=21; block=2; confidence=0.96 -->
The simulation process uses the same wire values and shares on $\hat I$. For each corrupted output wire, it forms the $t$ shares by the circuit’s linear combination, then interpolates the degree-$t$ polynomial with those shares and the functionality’s output as constant term.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p021-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=21; block=3; confidence=0.98 -->
For each corrupted output, the two constructed polynomials agree on $\hat I$, since both compute the same linear combination of the input and multiplication-output shares. Their constant terms also agree by circuit correctness, so the polynomials are equal. The first process represents the real execution; the second uses only corrupted outputs and represents the simulation. Together with Claim 4.3, this yields $\{S(I,\vec x_I,f_I(\vec x))\}\equiv\{\mathrm{view}^\pi_I(\vec x)\}$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p021-b004"></a>

## 5 Verifiable Secret Sharing (VSS)

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p028-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=28; block=2; confidence=1.00 -->
### 5.1 Background

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p028-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=28; block=3; confidence=0.99 -->
A threshold-\((t+1)\) secret-sharing scheme distributes a secret so any \(t+1\) shares recover it while any \(t\) or fewer reveal nothing. In Shamir sharing, a malicious dealer could use a polynomial of degree above \(t\), making reconstructions disagree; a malicious participant could submit a false share. Reconstruction should recover the secret from \(n-t\) correct shares despite up to \(t\) unknown incorrect shares. In multiparty computation, all \(n\) parties participate in reconstruction.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p028-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=28; block=4; confidence=0.99 -->
For \(t<n/3\), BGW VSS ensures honest parties receive shares \(q(\alpha_i)\) of one well-defined degree-\(t\) polynomial even with a corrupt dealer. Reed–Solomon decoding then recovers \(q\) from \(n-t\) correct shares despite up to \(t\) errors.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p028-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=28; block=5; confidence=1.00 -->
### 5.2 The Reed-Solomon Code

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p028-b006"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=28; block=6; confidence=1.00 -->
A linear \([n,k,d]\)-code over a field \(F\) of size \(q\) has codeword length \(n\), dimension \(k\) (hence \(q^k\) codewords), and minimum Hamming distance \(d\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p028-b007"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=28; block=7; confidence=0.99 -->
Choose a finite field \(F\) with \(|F|>n\) and distinct \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n\in F\). For message \(m=(m_0,\ldots,m_t)\in F^{t+1}\), define \(p_m(x)=m_0+m_1x+\cdots+m_tx^t\) and encode it as \(C(m)=\langle p_m(\alpha_1),\ldots,p_m(\alpha_n)\rangle\). The code has length \(n\), dimension \(t+1\), and distance \(n-t\): distinct degree-at-most-\(t\) polynomials agree at no more than \(t\) evaluation points.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p029-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=29; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
**Theorem 5.1.** The Reed–Solomon code is a linear \([n,t+1,n-t]\)-code over \(\mathbb F\). There exists an efficient decoding algorithm that corrects up to \(\frac{n-t-1}{2}\) errors: for every \(m\in\mathbb F^{t+1}\) and every \(x\in\mathbb F^n\) such that \(d(x,C(m))\le\frac{n-t-1}{2}\), the algorithm returns \(m\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p029-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=29; block=2; confidence=0.98 -->
If \(t<n/3\), then \(n\geq 3t+1\), so the decoder corrects at least \(\frac{(3t+1)-t-1}{2}=t\) errors.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p029-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=29; block=3; confidence=1.00 -->
### Reed–Solomon and Shamir secret-sharing

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p029-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=29; block=4; confidence=0.98 -->
**Reed–Solomon and Shamir’s secret-sharing.** The shares \(\{q(\alpha_i)\}_{i\in[n]}\), where \(q\in_R\mathcal P^{s,t}\) for secret \(s\in\mathbb F\), form a Reed–Solomon codeword. If at most \(t<n/3\) parties are corrupted, at most \(t\) symbols are incorrect; the Reed–Solomon reconstruction procedure corrects these errors, allowing honest parties to recover \(q\) and compute \(q(0)=s\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p029-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=29; block=5; confidence=1.00 -->
### 5.3 Bivariate Polynomials

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p029-b006"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=29; block=6; confidence=0.99 -->
A bivariate polynomial of degree \(t\) has degree at most \(t\) in each variable and can be written \(f(x,y)=\sum_{i=0}^{t}\sum_{j=0}^{t}a_{i,j}x^iy^j\). Let \(B_{s,t}\) be the set of such polynomials with constant term \(s\); it has \((t+1)^2-1=t^2+2t\) free coefficients.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p029-b007"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=29; block=7; confidence=0.99 -->
Fixing \(y=\alpha\) in a degree-\(t\) bivariate polynomial gives a degree-\(t\) univariate polynomial in \(x\). The text introduces the converse interpolation claim: \(t+1\) degree-\(t\) polynomials specified at distinct \(y\)-values determine one bivariate polynomial of degree \(t\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p030-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=30; block=1; confidence=1.00 -->
**Claim 5.2.** For a nonnegative integer \(t\), distinct \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_{t+1}\in F\), and degree-\(t\) polynomials \(f_1(x),\ldots,f_{t+1}(x)\), there is a unique degree-\(t\) bivariate polynomial \(S(x,y)\) satisfying \(S(x,\alpha_k)=f_k(x)\) for every \(k=1,\ldots,t+1\). (5.1)

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p030-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=30; block=2; confidence=0.99 -->
**Proof.** Define \(S\) by Lagrange interpolation: \[S(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{t+1} f_i(x)\frac{\prod_{1\leq j\leq t+1,\,j\ne i}(y-\alpha_j)}{\prod_{1\leq j\leq t+1,\,j\ne i}(\alpha_i-\alpha_j)}.\] This has degree at most \(t\) in each variable. At \(y=\alpha_k\), terms with \(i\ne k\) vanish and the \(i=k\) factor is 1, giving \[S(x,\alpha_k)=f_k(x)\frac{\prod_{j\ne k}(\alpha_k-\alpha_j)}{\prod_{j\ne k}(\alpha_k-\alpha_j)}+0=f_k(x),\] so (5.1) holds.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p030-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=30; block=3; confidence=0.99 -->
For uniqueness, suppose two degree-\(t\) bivariate polynomials \(S_1,S_2\) satisfy (5.1), and set \(R(x,y)=S_1(x,y)-S_2(x,y)=\sum_{i=0}^t\sum_{j=0}^t r_{i,j}x^iy^j\). For every \(k\in[t+1]\), \(R(x,\alpha_k)=0\). Regrouping by powers of \(x\), each coefficient polynomial \(h_i(y)=\sum_{j=0}^t r_{i,j}y^j\) vanishes at the \(t+1\) distinct points \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_{t+1}\), so \(h_i\) is the zero polynomial. Thus every \(r_{i,j}=0\), hence \(R=0\) and \(S_1=S_2\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p030-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=30; block=4; confidence=0.99 -->
Footnote 5: To see that all coefficients of a polynomial that is identically zero are zero, let \(p(x)=\sum_{i=0}^t a_i x^i\), where \(p(x)=0\) for every \(x\). For a vector \(\vec\beta\) of \(t+1\) distinct nonzero elements, its Vandermonde matrix satisfies \(V_{\vec\beta}\cdot\vec a=0\). Since \(V_{\vec\beta}\) is invertible, \(\vec a=V_{\vec\beta}^{-1}\cdot0=0\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p031-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=31; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
For each fixed index, the degree-$t$ polynomial $ h_i(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{t} r_{i,j}x^j $ vanishes at the $t+1$ distinct points $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_{t+1}$. Hence it is identically zero, so all coefficients $r_{i,j}$ vanish. Thus $R(x,y)=0$ everywhere and $S_1=S_2$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p031-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=31; block=2; confidence=0.99 -->
The protocol embeds a degree-$t$ Shamir polynomial $q(z)$ with $q(0)=s$ into a random bivariate degree-$t$ polynomial $S(x,y)$ constrained by $S(0,z)=q(z)$. Party $P_i$ receives $f_i(x)=S(x,\alpha_i)$ and $g_i(y)=S(\alpha_i,y)$, and outputs $f_i(0)=q(\alpha_i)$. Pairwise checks are $f_i(\alpha_j)=g_j(\alpha_i)$ and $g_i(\alpha_j)=f_j(\alpha_i)$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p031-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=31; block=3; confidence=0.99 -->
The next claim establishes that pairwise consistency determines a unique bivariate polynomial.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p031-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=31; block=4; confidence=0.98 -->
**Theorem 5.3.** Let $K\subseteq[n]$ have $|K|\ge t+1$. For degree-$t$ polynomials $\{f_k(x),g_k(y)\}_{k\in K}$ and distinct nonzero $\{\alpha_k\}_{k\in K}\subset F$, if $f_i(\alpha_j)=g_j(\alpha_i)$ for all $i,j\in K$, then there is a unique bivariate polynomial $S$ of degree at most $t$ in each variable satisfying $f_k(x)=S(x,\alpha_k)$ and $g_k(y)=S(\alpha_k,y)$ for every $k\in K$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p031-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=31; block=5; confidence=0.98 -->
**Claim 5.3 proof.** Let \(L\subseteq K\) have cardinality exactly \(t+1\). By Claim 5.2 there is a unique degree-\(t\) bivariate polynomial \(S(x,y)\) such that \(S(x,\alpha_\ell)=f_\ell(x)\) for each \(\ell\in L\). For \(k\in K\) and \(\ell\in L\), consistency gives \(g_k(\alpha_\ell)=f_\ell(\alpha_k)=S(\alpha_k,\alpha_\ell)\). The degree-\(t\) polynomials \(g_k(y)\) and \(S(\alpha_k,y)\) agree at \(t+1\) points, so they are equal. Also, for every \(j,k\in K\), \(f_k(\alpha_j)=g_j(\alpha_k)=S(\alpha_j,\alpha_k)\). Since \(|K|\ge t+1\), the degree-\(t\) polynomials \(f_k(x)\) and \(S(x,\alpha_k)\) agree at more than \(t\) points, hence are equal. This proves the claim.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p031-b006"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=31; block=6; confidence=0.97 -->
The text introduces a secrecy result: for at most $t$ corrupted indices, their received polynomial pairs have the same distribution for any two degree-$t$ polynomials agreeing on those indices, so the shares reveal no distinction between their constant terms.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p032-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=32; block=1; confidence=0.98 -->
**Theorem 5.4.** Let $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n\in F$ be distinct and nonzero, let $I\subseteq[n]$ with $|I|\le t$, and let degree-$t$ polynomials $q_1,q_2$ satisfy $q_1(\alpha_i)=q_2(\alpha_i)$ for every $i\in I$. If $S_1,S_2$ are sampled uniformly from degree-$t$ bivariate polynomials subject respectively to $S_1(0,z)=q_1(z)$ and $S_2(0,z)=q_2(z)$, then the distributions of $\{(i,S_1(x,\alpha_i),S_1(\alpha_i,y))\}_{i\in I}$ and $\{(i,S_2(x,\alpha_i),S_2(\alpha_i,y))\}_{i\in I}$ are identical.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p032-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=32; block=2; confidence=0.98 -->
**Proof.** Denote the two distributions on the corrupted parties' polynomial pairs by $\mathcal S_1$ and $\mathcal S_2$, sampled from the respective constrained bivariate-polynomial sets. It suffices to show each possible observation $Z=\{(i,f_i(x),g_i(y))\}_{i\in I}$ is equally likely under both.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p032-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=32; block=3; confidence=0.98 -->
For a set \(Z=\{(i,f_i(x),g_i(y))\}_{i\in I}\) satisfying \(f_i(\alpha_j)=g_j(\alpha_i)\) for all \(i,j\in I\) and \(f_i(0)=q_1(\alpha_i)=q_2(\alpha_i)\), the \(|I|\) fibers \(f_i\) are specified. Completing them to \(t+1\) fibers requires choosing \(t+1-|I|\) further degree-\(t\) polynomials. For each added fiber \(f_j\), its values at the \(|I|\) points \(\alpha_i\) and at \(0\) are fixed, leaving \(t-|I|\) values free. Thus the count displayed in the source is \((|\mathbb F|^{t-|I|})^{t+1-|I|}\); the same count applies under either secret condition.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p032-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=32; block=4; confidence=0.99 -->
Because each constrained bivariate polynomial is sampled uniformly, equal counts of polynomials consistent with every observation $Z$ imply equal probabilities for $Z$ under the two distributions. Thus $\mathcal S_1\equiv\mathcal S_2$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p033-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=33; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
Section 5.4 defines the BGW verifiable secret-sharing functionality and describes its protocol.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p033-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=33; block=2; confidence=0.98 -->
**Definition 5.5 (BGW VSS functionality).** On input polynomial $q(x)$ and $\lambda$ for the other parties, output $(q(\alpha_1),\ldots,q(\alpha_n))$ if $\deg(q)\le t$, and $(\bot,\ldots,\bot)$ otherwise. The shared secret is implicitly $s=q(0)$; sharing a chosen secret $s$ uses a random $q\in P_{s,t}$, where $P_{s,t}$ consists of degree-$t$ polynomials with constant term $s$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p033-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=33; block=3; confidence=0.97 -->
The dealer samples a degree-$t$ bivariate polynomial $S(x,y)$ with $S(0,z)=q(z)$ and sends $f_i(x)=S(x,\alpha_i)$ and $g_i(y)=S(\alpha_i,y)$ to each party. Claim 5.4 gives secrecy against up to $t$ corrupted parties; parties exchange evaluations to check $f_i(\alpha_j)=g_j(\alpha_i)$ and $g_i(\alpha_j)=f_j(\alpha_i)$. If checks pass, Claim 5.3 ensures all shares arise from one such $S$. For inconsistent values, a party broadcasts a complaint containing its received values; if the dealer's check finds them incorrect, the dealer broadcasts that party's two full polynomials for public consistency checks. Correct complaints do not trigger disclosure, preserving honest parties' shares. The continuation on the next page is not included.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p033-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=33; block=4; confidence=0.95 -->
The text begins arguing that an honest dealer gives consistent values to all honest parties, so complaints arise only from corrupted parties in that case; the argument continues beyond the supplied page. A footnote says this functionality explicitly outputs Shamir shares because later computation uses them, although VSS can be defined more abstractly.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p034-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=34; block=1; confidence=0.97 -->
The preceding discussion says complaint resolution must preserve security: an honest dealer can reveal consistent data when needed, while an dishonest dealer must resend consistent polynomials or honest parties reject. Details are deferred to Protocol 5.6.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p035-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=35; block=1; confidence=1.00 -->
**Protocol 5.6.**

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p035-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=35; block=2; confidence=0.97 -->
The dealer is party $P_1$ and holds a polynomial $q(x)$ of degree at most $t$; an honest dealer aborts if this bound fails. Parties $P_2,\ldots,P_n$ have no input. Public data are a field $F$ and nonzero, distinct-looking (distinctness not stated) elements $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n\in F$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p035-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=35; block=3; confidence=0.98 -->
**Protocol 5.6, Round 1.** The dealer selects uniformly at random a bivariate polynomial \(S(x,y)\in_R\mathcal B^{q(0),t}\) subject to \(S(0,z)=q(z)\). For each \(i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\), it defines \(f_i(x)=S(x,\alpha_i)\) and \(g_i(y)=S(\alpha_i,y)\), and sends \(f_i(x),g_i(y)\) to \(P_i\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p035-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=35; block=4; confidence=0.99 -->
Each $P_i$ stores its received $f_i,g_i$, truncating either polynomial to degree $t$ if needed, then sends $f_i(\alpha_j)$ and $g_i(\alpha_j)$ to each $P_j$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p035-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=35; block=5; confidence=0.96 -->
**Protocol 5.6, Rounds 3–5.** Party \(P_i\) compares the values \((u_j,v_j)\) received from \(P_j\) with the expected values \((f_j(\alpha_i),g_j(\alpha_i))\). If \(u_j\ne f_j(\alpha_i)\) or \(v_j\ne g_j(\alpha_i)\), it broadcasts \(\operatorname{complaint}(i,j,f_i(\alpha_j),g_i(\alpha_j))\). If there are no complaints, every party outputs \(f_i(0)\) and halts.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p035-b006"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=35; block=6; confidence=0.96 -->
For each broadcast $\operatorname{complaint}(i,j,u,v)$, the dealer checks whether $u=S(\alpha_j,\alpha_i)$ and $v=S(\alpha_i,\alpha_j)$. If both hold it takes no action; otherwise it broadcasts $\operatorname{reveal}(i,f_i(x),g_i(y))$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p035-b007"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=35; block=7; confidence=0.95 -->
Party $P_i$ marks $(j,k)$ as a joint complaint when it sees $\operatorname{complaint}(k,j,u_1,v_1)$ and $\operatorname{complaint}(j,k,u_2,v_2)$ with $u_1\ne v_2$ or $v_1\ne u_2$. If some such pair has neither party's reveal from the dealer, $P_i$ skips to the output decision without broadcasting `consistent`.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p035-b008"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=35; block=8; confidence=0.91 -->
After truncating revealed polynomials to degree $t$ as in Round 2, party $P_i$ resets its stored pair and skips `consistent` if a reveal for $i$ appears. It also skips `consistent` if a reveal for $j\ne i$ conflicts with its stored pair, namely if $f_i(\alpha_j)\ne g_j(\alpha_i)$ or $g_i(\alpha_j)\ne f_j(\alpha_i)$. If neither condition applies, it broadcasts `consistent`.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p035-b009"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=35; block=9; confidence=0.99 -->
When complaints occurred, each party outputs $f_i(0)$ if at least $n-t$ parties broadcast `consistent`; otherwise it outputs $\bot$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p036-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=36; block=1; confidence=1.00 -->
The discussion motivates the protocol's security before the formal theorem.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p036-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=36; block=2; confidence=0.93 -->
With an honest dealer, all shares are pairwise consistent: $f_i(\alpha_j)=g_j(\alpha_i)$ and $f_j(\alpha_i)=g_i(\alpha_j)$. Complaints can arise from corrupted parties' incorrect messages, whose contents are already known to the adversary; any reveal of an honest party's shares is unnecessary, while a correct reveal for a corrupted complainant adds no information. Honest parties therefore pass the reveal checks and at least $n-t$ broadcast `consistent`, outputting $f_j(0)=S(0,\alpha_j)=q(\alpha_j)$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p036-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=36; block=3; confidence=0.96 -->
In the corrupted-dealer case, if at least \(n-t\) parties broadcast consistent, then at least \((n-t)-t=t+1\) honest parties broadcast consistent. By Claim 5.3, their polynomials are derived from a unique degree-\(t\) bivariate polynomial \(S(x,y)\). The argument then shows that every other honest party’s polynomials are derived from the same \(S\): if \(f_j(x)\ne S(x,\alpha_j)\), they agree at at most \(t\) honest consistent parties, forcing a joint complaint that would have prevented one of those parties from broadcasting consistent. The analogous argument applies to \(g_j(y)\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p036-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=36; block=4; confidence=1.00 -->
**Theorem 5.7.** If $t<n/3$, Protocol 5.6 is $t$-secure for the FV SS functionality against a static malicious adversary.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p036-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=36; block=5; confidence=0.97 -->
**Proof.** Given any real-world adversary $A$, construct a simulator $\mathrm{SIM}$ such that for every corruption set $I$ and every input, the parties' and adversary's outputs in the real execution equal those in the ideal execution with $\mathrm{SIM}$. The proof separates cases according to whether the dealer is honest; it continues beyond the supplied page.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p037-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=37; block=1; confidence=1.00 -->
## Case 1: Honest dealer

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p037-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=37; block=2; confidence=0.99 -->
In the ideal execution, each honest party receives and outputs its share $q(\alpha_j)$. The real-execution argument must show the same result and construct a simulator using the corrupted parties’ outputs $q(\alpha_i)$, $i\in I$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p037-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=37; block=3; confidence=0.99 -->
An honest party $P_j$ can output only $f_j(0)=S(0,\alpha_j)=q(\alpha_j)$ or $\bot$. Its polynomial is unchanged because an honest dealer does not reveal a replacement for $P_j$’s polynomials. It avoids $\bot$ exactly when at least $n-t$ parties broadcast `consistent`; hence it suffices to show every honest party broadcasts `consistent`.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p037-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=37; block=4; confidence=0.98 -->
A party $P_j$ broadcasts `consistent` provided: (1) for each conflicting pair of complaints `complaint(k, ℓ, u₁, v₁)` and `complaint(ℓ, k, u₂, v₂)` with $u_1\ne v_2$ and $v_1\ne u_2$, the dealer reveals $k$ or $\ell$ (or both) in Step 4a; (2) the dealer does not reveal $P_j$’s pair $(f_j(x),g_j(y))$; and (3) every reveal of $(f_k(x),g_k(y))$ satisfies $g_k(\alpha_j)=f_j(\alpha_k)$ and $f_k(\alpha_j)=g_j(\alpha_k)$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p037-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=37; block=5; confidence=0.98 -->
With an honest dealer, a conflict forces at least one reveal: both complaint pairs cannot agree with the same bivariate polynomial $S$, so the dealer’s rule resolves the conflict. Revealed polynomials satisfy condition (3) by honesty. A reveal of $P_j$’s polynomials occurs only after an incorrect complaint from $P_j$, which cannot occur when both $P_j$ and the dealer are honest. Thus all honest parties broadcast `consistent`, and output $f_j(0)=q(\alpha_j)$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p038-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=38; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
**Simulator.** Invoke $A(z)$ and receive $\{q(\alpha_i):i\in I\}$ from the trusted party. Choose a polynomial $q'(x)$ agreeing with $q$ at every $\alpha_i$ for $i\in I$, run the honest parties and dealer against $A$ using $q'$ as the dealer input, then output whatever $A$ outputs.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p038-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=38; block=2; confidence=0.97 -->
The corrupted parties’ view after Round 1 is determined by their polynomial pairs $\{(f_i(x),g_i(y)):i\in I\}$. Honest-party complaints against corrupted parties contain the correct values $(f_j(\alpha_i),g_j(\alpha_i))=(g_i(\alpha_j),f_i(\alpha_j))$, and any dealer reveals are only of corrupted parties’ polynomials. The same dependence holds in the real execution; Claim 5.4 states that these polynomial-pair distributions are identical for $q$ and $q'$. Thus the simulated view has the required distribution.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p038-b003"></a>

## Case 2: Corrupted dealer

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p038-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=38; block=4; confidence=0.97 -->
The simulator plays all honest parties. If fewer than $n-t$ parties broadcast `consistent`, it will submit an invalid polynomial to the trusted party; otherwise it reconstructs a degree-$t$ polynomial from the honest parties’ consistent shares and submits that polynomial’s constant-variable slice.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p038-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=38; block=5; confidence=0.99 -->
**Simulator.** (1) Invoke $A(z)$. (2) Play the $n-|I|$ honest parties according to the protocol through its end.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p039-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=39; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
**Simulator (continued).** (3) Let $\mathrm{num}$ count all parties broadcasting `consistent`. If $\mathrm{num}<n-t$, send $q'(x)=x^{2t}$ to the trusted party, causing its output to be $\bot$. If $\mathrm{num}\ge n-t$, let $K\subseteq[n]\setminus I$ be the honest parties broadcasting `consistent`; use Claim 5.3 to obtain the guaranteed unique degree-$t$ bivariate polynomial $S$ for $K$, set $q'(x)=S(0,x)$, and send $q'$ to the trusted party. Receive the corrupted parties’ values $\{q'(\alpha_i):i\in I\}$ (unused). (4) Output whatever $A$ outputs and halt.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p039-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=39; block=2; confidence=0.98 -->
All parties and the simulator are deterministic in this case; the adversary may be taken deterministic by including its attack coins in $z$. Since the simulator follows the protocol for every honest party, its messages to $A$ match those in a real execution, so $A$’s outputs coincide. It remains to prove equality of the honest parties’ outputs.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p039-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=39; block=3; confidence=0.98 -->
**Theorem (Claim 5.8).** Let $J=[n]\setminus I$ be the honest-party indices. For every adversary $A$ controlling $I$ (including the dealer), polynomial $q(x)$, and auxiliary input $z\in\{0,1\}^*$,
\[
\operatorname{output}^{J}\!\left(\mathrm{REAL}_{\pi,A(z),I}(q(x),\lambda,\ldots,\lambda)\right)=\operatorname{output}^{J}\!\left(\mathrm{IDEAL}_{\mathrm{F}_{VSS},S(z),I}(q(x),\lambda,\ldots,\lambda)\right).
\]

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p039-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=39; block=4; confidence=0.96 -->
**Proof.** Put $\vec{x}=(q(x),\lambda,\ldots,\lambda)$. Split into cases according to whether some honest party outputs $\bot$ in the real execution. The first case begins with an honest $P_j$ outputting $\bot$; the proof aims to show that then all honest parties output $\bot$ in both executions. By the protocol, $P_j$ outputs $\bot$ exactly when it receives fewer than $n-t$ `consistent` messages. The argument continues beyond the supplied page.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p040-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=40; block=1; confidence=0.98 -->
If an honest party outputs \(\bot\) in the real execution, fewer than \(n-t\) parties broadcast consistent messages. Since broadcasts are shared, all honest parties output \(\bot\). In the simulation, the same messages imply \(\mathrm{num}<n-t\), so SIM sends \(q(x)=x^{2t}\) to the trusted party; hence all honest parties output \(\bot\) in the ideal execution too.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p040-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=40; block=2; confidence=0.97 -->
Suppose every honest party outputs something other than \(\bot\) in the real execution. Then at least \(n-t\ge 2t+1\) parties broadcast consistent messages, including at least \(t+1\) honest parties. For each such party \(P_j\), the dealer resolved conflicts, sent no \(\mathrm{reveal}(j,f_j(x),g_j(y))\), and the revealed polynomials fit \(P_j\)'s polynomials. Thus for the honest consistent set \(K\), \(f_i(\alpha_j)=g_j(\alpha_i)\) for all \(i,j\in K\). Claim 5.3 gives a unique bivariate polynomial \(S\) with \(S(x,\alpha_k)=f_k(x)\) and \(S(\alpha_k,y)=g_k(y)\) for every \(k\in K\). Consequently \(q'(x)=S(0,x)\) is uniquely determined, and SIM sends \(q'\) to the trusted party, so honest ideal outputs are \(q'(\alpha_j)\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p040-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=40; block=3; confidence=0.94 -->
The transcript determines the same \(q'\) in real and ideal executions. Every honest \(P_k\) with \(k\in K\) outputs \(f_k(0)\), which equals \(q'(\alpha_k)\) because \(q'\) is the unique polynomial through the points \((\alpha_k,f_k(0))\). The proof then considers an honest \(P_j\notin K\), using its post-replacement polynomials \(f'_j,g'_j\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p041-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=41; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
Since all conflicts are properly resolved, for each \(k\in K\) we have \(f'_j(\alpha_k)=g_k(\alpha_j)\). As the consistent parties’ polynomials come from \(S\), this equals \(S(\alpha_k,\alpha_j)\). Thus \(f'_j(x)\) and \(S(x,\alpha_j)\), both of degree \(t\), agree at \(t+1\) points and are equal. Hence \(f'_j(0)=S(0,\alpha_j)=q'(\alpha_j)\), completing the proof of Theorem 5.7.

With no malicious parties and no complaints, the protocol ends after Round 3: the dealer sends each party two polynomials, and each party sends two field elements to every other party.

### 6.1 High-Level Overview

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p041-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=41; block=5; confidence=0.97 -->
The section targets secure multiplication of shared values against up to \(t<n/3\) malicious parties. Given degree-\(t\) shares \(a_i,b_i\), the products \(a_i b_i\) lie on a degree-\(2t\) polynomial whose constant term is \(ab\). The semi-honest approach rerandomizes this polynomial, then reduces its degree while preserving the constant term. The simplification in [18] combines those stages: parties subshare each \(a_i b_i\), then locally combine the resulting subshares to obtain a random degree-\(t\) polynomial hiding \(ab\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p041-b006"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=41; block=6; confidence=0.99 -->
For \(n=3t+1\), a Reed–Solomon code for degree-\(2t\) shares has parameters \([n,2t+1,n-2t]\) and corrects up to \(\frac{n-2t-1}{2}=\frac{t}{2}\) errors. This is insufficient when there are \(t\) corrupted parties.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p042-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=42; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
The BGW protocol proceeds in three steps:

1. Parties distribute verifiable subshares of their input shares \(a_i\) and \(b_i\). These are degree-\(t\) shares, forming a Reed–Solomon code with parameters \([n,t+1,n-t]\), which corrects up to \(t\) errors. This uses \(F^{\mathrm{subshare}}_{VSS}\).
2. Parties distribute subshares of \(a_i\cdot b_i\). This protocol verifies that the distributed values are the correct products using the separate subshares of \(a_i\) and \(b_i\). It uses \(F^{\mathrm{mult}}_{VSS}\), introduced in Section 6.6, and the functionality \(F_{\mathrm{eval}}\), introduced in Section 6.5.
3. Parties locally compute a linear combination of their verified degree-\(t\) subshares of all products \(a_i\cdot b_i\), obtaining shares of \(a\cdot b\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p042-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=42; block=2; confidence=0.96 -->
Sections 6.2–6.3 introduce corruption-aware functionalities and a subprotocol for matrix multiplication over a shared vector; the latter supports the construction of \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{subshare}}^{\mathrm{VSS}}\) in Section 6.4.

### 6.2 Corruption-Aware Functionalities and Their Use

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p042-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=42; block=4; confidence=0.94 -->
A standard secure-computation functionality specifies inputs and outputs. The section extends this model with functionalities whose behavior may depend on which parties are corrupted, granting the adversary additional power in selected cases; the BGW input-sharing phase motivates this extension.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p042-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=42; block=5; confidence=0.96 -->
For \(t<n/4\) (equivalently \(n\ge4t+1\)), degree-\(2t\) product shares can be error-corrected directly, allowing product subsharing without the Section 6.6 multiplication subprotocol. Appendix A describes this variant and assumes familiarity with Sections 6.2, 6.3, 6.4, and 6.7.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p043-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=43; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
**Functionality 6.1 (naive parallel VSS, $F_{
m VSS}^n$).** Receive polynomials $q_1(x),\ldots,q_n(x)$ from $P_1,\ldots,P_n$. Replace $q_i(x)$ by $\bot$ if it was not submitted or if $\deg(q_i)>t$. For each $i=1,\ldots,n$, send $P_i$ the vector $(q_1(\alpha_i),\ldots,q_n(\alpha_i))$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p043-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=43; block=2; confidence=0.98 -->
A rushing adversary sees corrupted parties’ first-round shares of honest polynomials before choosing its own polynomials. Thus, for honest $P_j$ and corrupted $P_i$, it can choose $q_i$ so that $q_i(\alpha_i)=q_j(\alpha_i)$. In the ideal execution of Functionality 6.1, corrupted inputs are fixed before the adversary learns those shares, so this dependence cannot be reproduced. Therefore, $n$ parallel executions of the single-party VSS protocol do not securely realize this functionality. Although this dependence may be harmless in applications where $q_j(\alpha_i)$ is independent of $q_j(0)$, it remains a mismatch between the real and ideal models.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p043-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=43; block=3; confidence=0.98 -->
One alternative is to execute VSS invocations sequentially and let each party choose its polynomial after receiving its shares from earlier invocations. This permits the ideal functionality to model the dependence, but the resulting protocol is not constant-round.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p043-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=43; block=4; confidence=0.96 -->
A second alternative preserves parallel execution and changes the ideal functionality so corrupted parties choose their polynomials after seeing their shares of honest polynomials; the formal definition follows.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p044-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=44; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
**Functionality 6.2 (corruption-aware parallel VSS, $F_{
m VSS}^n$).** Receive the corrupted-party index set $I\subseteq[n]$. First receive $q_j(x)$ from every honest party $P_j$ with $j\notin I$. Send the ideal adversary the shares $\{q_j(\alpha_i):j\notin I\}$ for each $i\in I$. Then receive $q_i(x)$ from the adversary for each $i\in I$. Finally, send each $P_j$, for $j=1,\ldots,n$, the vector $(q_1(\alpha_j),\ldots,q_n(\alpha_j))$, using $\bot$ in place of $q_i(\alpha_j)$ whenever $\deg(q_i(x))>t$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p044-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=44; block=2; confidence=0.98 -->
This functionality models the adversary’s ability to choose corrupted inputs based on observed shares. To formalize it, the security definition is extended so a functionality receives the corrupted set as auxiliary input; honest parties do not learn that set.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p044-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=44; block=3; confidence=0.98 -->
**Definition 6.3.** Let \(f:2^{[n]}\times(\{0,1\}^*)^n\to(\{0,1\}^*)^n\) be a corruption-aware \(n\)-ary functionality and \(\pi\) a protocol. We say that \(\pi\) is \(t\)-secure for \(f\) if for every probabilistic real-model adversary \(A\), there exists a probabilistic ideal-model adversary \(S\) of comparable complexity such that for every \(I\subseteq[n]\) with \(|I|\le t\), every \(\vec x\in(\{0,1\}^*)^n\) with \(|x_1|=\cdots=|x_n|\), and every \(z\in\{0,1\}^*\), \(\{\operatorname{IDEAL}_{f_I,S(z),I}(\vec x)\}\) is distributed identically to \(\{\operatorname{REAL}_{\pi,A(z),I}(\vec x)\}\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p044-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=44; block=4; confidence=0.97 -->
Because adversaries are static, $I$ is determined before execution. The accompanying footnote observes that Functionality 6.2 could send final shares only to honest parties, since corrupted parties already received them; the definition sends them to all parties for clarity.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p045-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=45; block=1; confidence=0.96 -->
Making functionality behavior depend on corrupted identities was introduced to increase flexibility and is widely used in universal composability.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p045-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=45; block=2; confidence=0.98 -->
**Hybrid execution convention.** An execution in the $G_I$-hybrid model, denoted $\operatorname{HYBRID}^{G_I}_{f,A(z),I}(\vec{x})$, is parameterized by corrupted set $I$, which is supplied to functionality $G$ at initialization. The set is fixed before execution; honest parties do not know it, and their protocol messages and inputs to the functionality are independent of it.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p045-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=45; block=3; confidence=0.98 -->
The ideal functionality’s behavior may depend on $I$; real protocol code is fixed independently of $I$. The hybrid model keeps honest-party code fixed while allowing its aiding functionality to be determined using $I$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p045-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=45; block=4; confidence=0.98 -->
An ordinary functionality $f$ can be represented as a corruption-aware one that ignores $I$. Security for this representation implies security for $f$ in the standard model, including when the protocol uses corruption-aware subprotocols: the simulator knows $I$ in either model, and the corresponding ideal ensembles are identical, $\operatorname{IDEAL}_{f_I,S(z),I}(\vec{x})=\operatorname{IDEAL}_{f,S(z),I}(\vec{x})$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p045-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=45; block=5; confidence=0.96 -->
The cited sequential modular-composition theorems do not state corruption-aware functionalities, but their proofs extend to that setting.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p046-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=46; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
Corruption-aware ideal functionalities permit explicitly modeled extra information or treatment for corrupted parties, making their advantage well-defined. The approach supports modular analysis of BGW subprotocols, although BGW’s final general computation result concerns ordinary functionalities: a circuit receiving only parties’ inputs cannot generally implement functionalities that depend on the corrupted-party set. Some such functionalities, such as announcing which parties are corrupted, are unrealizable because corrupted parties can behave honestly. Corruption awareness can also simplify functionality definitions, for example by reconstructing from honest parties’ shares alone.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p047-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=47; block=1; confidence=0.98 -->
The ideal adversary communicates directly with a corruption-aware functionality on behalf of corrupted parties; this is equivalent to adversarially chosen inputs from those parties. The notation often suppresses the corrupted set \(I\), but each such functionality takes \(I\) as auxiliary input, and aiding ideal functionalities receive \(I\) at initialization.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p047-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=47; block=2; confidence=1.00 -->
## 6.3 Matrix Multiplication in the Presence of Malicious Adversaries

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p047-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=47; block=3; confidence=0.99 -->
**6.3 Matrix Multiplication in the Presence of Malicious Adversaries.** For a fixed matrix \(A\in\mathbb F^{n\times m}\), party \(P_i\) holds \(x_i\in\mathbb F\); the parties compute \((x_1,\ldots,x_n)A\), and every party receives the whole output vector. The functionality is linear, so parties can share inputs, compute the linear function locally on shares, and reconstruct the output. In the malicious setting, verifiable secret sharing is used for input sharing and Reed–Solomon decoding for output reconstruction.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p047-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=47; block=4; confidence=0.99 -->
Each \(P_i\) chooses a random polynomial \(g_i\) with \(g_i(0)=x_i\) and shares it via \(F_{VSS}\). After sharing, \(P_i\) holds \(g_1(\alpha_i),\ldots,g_n(\alpha_i)\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p047-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=47; block=5; confidence=0.98 -->
Party \(P_i\) computes \(\vec y_i=(g_1(\alpha_i),\ldots,g_n(\alpha_i))A\). Its \(j\)-th coordinate is \(y_{i,j}=\sum_{\ell=1}^n g_\ell(\alpha_i)a_{\ell,j}\); thus the \(y_{i,j}\) are Shamir shares of \(y_j=\sum_{\ell=1}^n g_\ell(0)a_{\ell,j}\), the \(j\)-th coordinate of \((g_1(0),\ldots,g_n(0))A\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p048-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=48; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
Each party \(P_i\) sends its vector \(\vec y^{\,i}\) to all other parties. Each party then reconstructs \(\vec y=(g_1(0),\ldots,g_n(0))\cdot A\) by error correction. For the \(j\)th coordinate, the values sent are \(\sum_{\ell=1}^n g_\ell(\alpha_i)a_{\ell,j}\), evaluations of a degree-\(t\) polynomial whose constant term is \(\sum_{\ell=1}^n g_\ell(0)a_{\ell,j}\). Reed–Solomon error correction on each column corrects incorrect shares.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p048-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=48; block=2; confidence=0.99 -->
VSS prevents a corrupted party from sharing an invalid input polynomial, and output error correction prevents corrupted parties from changing the reconstructed result.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p048-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=48; block=3; confidence=0.97 -->
For degree-\(t\) input polynomials \(g_1,\ldots,g_n\), define \(\vec y=(g_1(0),\ldots,g_n(0))A\). The proposed functionality gives each \(P_i\) output \((\vec y,\{g_\ell(\alpha_i)\}_{\ell=1}^n)\), collectively written \(F^A_{mat}(g_1,\ldots,g_n)=\big((\vec y,\{g_\ell(\alpha_1)\}_{\ell=1}^n),\ldots,(\vec y,\{g_\ell(\alpha_n)\}_{\ell=1}^n)\big)\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p048-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=48; block=4; confidence=0.98 -->
With polynomial inputs, each honest party’s sent output-share vector is determined by the input polynomials. If only \(P_1\) is corrupted, the ideal adversary receives \(\vec y\) and the shares at \(\alpha_1\), while in the real protocol it sees the honest parties’ vectors \(\vec y_2,\ldots,\vec y_n\); the simulator cannot fabricate these as random shares because they are fixed by the inputs. The text notes that the next section addresses this with a corruption-aware variant.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p049-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=49; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
The protocol messages are deterministic from the input polynomials and matrix, so a simulator lacking the polynomials cannot reproduce them exactly. The text illustrates this with random polynomials and the identity matrix.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p049-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=49; block=2; confidence=0.96 -->
One solution is to modify the protocol by adding randomness so that the vectors \(\vec y^{\,i}\) are not deterministic functions of the inputs. The text explains that this is unnecessary for securely computing \(F^{\mathrm{subshare}}_{VSS}\): security is maintained even if the adversary learns the vector of polynomials \(\vec Y(x)=(Y_1(x),\ldots,Y_m(x))=(g_1(x),\ldots,g_n(x))\cdot A\), because \(\vec Y(0)\) is the basic output.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p049-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=49; block=3; confidence=0.96 -->
Before formally defining \(F^A_{\mathrm{mat}}\), the text explains that corruption awareness also models the adversary’s ability to choose corrupted parties’ polynomials after seeing their shares of the honest parties’ polynomials.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p049-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=49; block=4; confidence=0.99 -->
Parallel VSS lets the adversary choose corrupted parties’ polynomials after seeing their shares of honest parties’ polynomials; the functionality models this ordering.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p049-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=49; block=5; confidence=0.99 -->
**Functionality 6.4.** For \(A\in F^{n\times m}\), \(F^A_{\mathrm{mat}}\) receives corrupted index set \(I\subseteq[n]\). It receives honest parties’ polynomials \(g_j(x)\), \(j\notin I\), resetting missing or degree-\(>t\) inputs to zero. It sends \(g_j(\alpha_i)\) for \(j\notin I, i\in I\) to the ideal adversary, then receives corrupted parties’ \(g_i(x)\), resetting missing or degree-\(>t\) inputs to zero. It computes \(\vec Y(x)=(Y_1(x),\ldots,Y_m(x))=(g_1(x),\ldots,g_n(x))A\). Each honest party \(P_j\) receives \(\vec y=\vec Y(0)\) and its shares \((g_1(\alpha_j),\ldots,g_n(\alpha_j))\). The adversary receives \(\vec Y(x)\) and, for each \(i\in I\), the output \(\vec y\) together with \((g_1(\alpha_i),\ldots,g_n(\alpha_i))\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p050-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=50; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
The protocol and security proof are given in the corruption-aware hybrid model, using the parallel VSS functionality.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p050-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=50; block=2; confidence=0.99 -->
**Protocol 6.5.** In the \(F^n_{\mathrm{VSS}}\)-hybrid model, parties with polynomials \(g_i(x)\) share common field \(F\), distinct nonzero \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n\), and matrix \(A\in F^{n\times m}\). Each party resets a non-degree-\(t\) input polynomial to zero and privately submits it to parallel VSS. Party \(P_i\) receives \(g_1(\alpha_i),\ldots,g_n(\alpha_i)\), replacing any \(\bot\) by zero, forms \(\vec x_i=(g_1(\alpha_i),\ldots,g_n(\alpha_i))\), and computes \(\vec y_i=\vec x_iA\), equivalently \(Y_k(\alpha_i)=\sum_{\ell=1}^n g_\ell(\alpha_i)a_{\ell,k}\). It sends \(\vec y_i\) to every party. For each sender index \(j\), it treats the received vector as \(\widehat{\vec Y}(\alpha_j)\), replacing missing entries by zero, then Reed–Solomon decodes each column with distance parameter \(d=2t+1\), reconstructs \(Y_k(x)\), and outputs \((Y_1(0),\ldots,Y_m(0))\) together with \(g_1(\alpha_i),\ldots,g_n(\alpha_i)\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p050-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=50; block=3; confidence=0.99 -->
The received vectors form a matrix; columnwise error correction recovers the output codewords.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p051-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=51; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
**Theorem 6.6.** If \(t<n/3\), Protocol 6.5 is \(t\)-secure for \(F^A_{\mathrm{mat}}\) in the \(F_{\mathrm{VSS}}\)-hybrid model against a static malicious adversary.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p051-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=51; block=2; confidence=0.99 -->
**Proof.** The simulator \(S\) interacts with the ideal \(F^A_{\mathrm{mat}}\) externally and invokes the hybrid adversary \(A\) internally, simulating honest-party messages and the adversary’s VSS interaction.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p051-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=51; block=3; confidence=0.97 -->
**Simulator \(S\).** It invokes \(A\) with auxiliary input \(z\). After the honest parties submit their inputs to \(F^A_{\mathrm{mat}}\), it receives shares \(\{g_j(\alpha_i)\}_{j\notin I,\,i\in I}\). It internally simulates the ideal \(F^n_{VSS}\) invocation, gives \(A\) those shares, receives the corrupted parties’ polynomials \(g_i(x)\), replacing any with degree greater than \(t\) by zero, and provides the expected share outputs, using \(\bot\) where a polynomial has degree greater than \(t\). It sends the corrupted polynomials to \(F^A_{\mathrm{mat}}\), then receives \(\vec Y(x)=(g_1(x),\ldots,g_n(x))\cdot A\), the output vector \(\vec y\), and the corrupted parties’ outputs. For each \(j\notin I\) and \(i\in I\), it gives \(P_i\) the message \(\vec y^{\,j}=(Y_1(\alpha_j),\ldots,Y_m(\alpha_j))\). Finally, it outputs whatever \(A\) outputs and halts.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p052-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=52; block=1; confidence=0.98 -->
**Proof.** For each \(I\subseteq[n]\) with \(|I|\le t\), the ideal and protocol output distributions in (6.1) agree. In the FVSS-hybrid model the honest parties, simulator, and ideal functionality act deterministically, so it suffices to match the adversary’s view and the honest outputs. The corrupted parties’ shares of the honest parties’ polynomials, together with \(\vec Y(x)\) given to the simulator, determine the messages they receive; hence the views match. The honest parties compute the same \(\vec Y(x)\) as the functionality: honest polynomial components agree, and matching adversary views imply the same corrupted-party polynomials \(g_i(x)\). For each component \(k\), the word \((\widehat Y_k(\alpha_1),\ldots,\widehat Y_k(\alpha_n))\) agrees with \(Y_k(\alpha_j)\) for every \(j\notin I\), giving at least \(n-t\) correct entries. Reed–Solomon decoding therefore reconstructs the functionality’s \(Y_k(x)\), regardless of corrupted-party messages.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p052-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=52; block=2; confidence=0.99 -->
## 6.4 The FVSS Functionality for Sharing Shares

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p052-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=52; block=3; confidence=0.97 -->
The subsharing functionality distributes each party’s share \(f(\alpha_i)\) through degree-\(t\) polynomials. In the malicious setting it must ensure that a corrupted party’s polynomial has constant term equal to its prescribed share, where \(f\) is determined by the honest parties’ shares (there are more than \(t\) honest parties).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p053-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=53; block=1; confidence=0.98 -->
If a corrupted party fails to provide a degree-\(t\) polynomial \(g_i(x)\) with \(g_i(0)=f(\alpha_i)\), the functionality substitutes the constant polynomial \(g'_i(x)=f(\alpha_i)\). It is corruption-aware: the adversary can choose corrupted parties’ polynomials after seeing shares of honest parties’ polynomials sent to corrupted parties.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p053-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=53; block=2; confidence=0.95 -->
The protocol invokes the matrix multiplication functionality with \(H^T\), where \(H\) is the Reed–Solomon parity-check matrix; its adversary output includes \(\vec Y(x)=(g_1(x),\ldots,g_n(x))H^T\). The subsharing functionality therefore also reveals this vector to the ideal adversary. For this \(H\), the constant vector \((g_1(0),\ldots,g_n(0))H^T\) is determined by corrupted inputs and honest parties’ prescribed values, while the polynomial vector \((g_1(x),\ldots,g_n(x))H^T\) is random subject to that constraint, allowing simulation.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p054-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=54; block=1; confidence=0.98 -->
**Definition (Functionality 6.7: \(F^{\mathrm{VSS}}_{\mathrm{subshare}}\) for subsharing shares).** Given corrupted set \(I\subseteq[n]\): (1) receive honest inputs \(\{\beta_j\}_{j\notin I}\) and let \(f(x)\) be the unique degree-\(t\) polynomial through \((\alpha_j,\beta_j)\) for \(j\notin I\); (2) for each honest \(j\), choose random degree-\(t\) \(g_j(x)\) subject to \(g_j(0)=\beta_j=f(\alpha_j)\); (3) send \(\{g_j(\alpha_i):j\notin I,i\in I\}\) to the ideal adversary; (4) receive \(g_i(x)\) from each corrupted \(i\), replacing missing or degree-\(>t\) polynomials by zero; (5) set \(g'_j=g_j\) for honest \(j\), and for corrupted \(i\) set \(g'_i=g_i\) if \(g_i(0)=f(\alpha_i)\), otherwise set \(g'_i\) to the constant polynomial \(f(\alpha_i)\); (6a) send each honest \(P_j\) the polynomial \(g'_j(x)\) and shares \((g'_1(\alpha_j),\ldots,g'_n(\alpha_j))\); (6b) send the adversary \(\vec Y(x)=(g_1(x),\ldots,g_n(x))H^T\), the corrupted outputs \(g'_i(x)\), and \((g'_1(\alpha_i),\ldots,g'_n(\alpha_i))\) for each \(i\in I\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p054-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=54; block=2; confidence=0.98 -->
For a generalized Reed–Solomon code with length \(n=3t+1\), dimension \(k=t+1\), and distance \(d=2t+1\), let \(G\in F^{(t+1)\times n}\) be the generator matrix. Encoding coefficient vector \(\vec a=(a_0,\ldots,a_t)\) is \(\vec aG\), with

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p054-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=54; block=3; confidence=0.98 -->
The generator matrix is \(G\in\mathbb F^{(t+1)\times n}\), with columns \((1,\alpha_i,\ldots,\alpha_i^t)^T\), for \(i=1,\ldots,n\). Its encoding maps coefficient vector \((a_0,\ldots,a_t)\) to \((f(\alpha_1),\ldots,f(\alpha_n))\), where \(f(x)=\sum_{\ell=0}^t a_\ell x^\ell\). Let \(H\in\mathbb F^{2t\times n}\) be a rank-\(2t\) parity-check matrix with \(GH^T=0\). For \(\vec\beta\in\mathbb F^n\), the syndrome is \(S(\vec\beta)=\vec\beta H^T\); it vanishes on codewords and satisfies \(S(\vec\beta+\vec e)=S(\vec e)\). Errors of weight at most \(t\) can be corrected. Footnote 11 notes that if honest points do not lie on one degree-\(t\) polynomial, no security guarantee applies; the functionality may be formalized as partial, or may reveal honest inputs and allow the adversary to determine honest outputs.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p055-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=55; block=1; confidence=0.98 -->
A Reed–Solomon decoding subprocedure recovers the error vector \(\mathbf e\) from syndrome \(S(\mathbf e)\). For a received word \(\boldsymbol\gamma=\boldsymbol\beta+\mathbf e\), its syndrome is \(S(\boldsymbol\gamma)=\boldsymbol\gamma H^T=S(\mathbf e)\), allowing recovery of \(\boldsymbol\beta\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p055-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=55; block=2; confidence=0.99 -->
## The protocol

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p055-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=55; block=3; confidence=0.98 -->
Each party \(P_i\) chooses a random degree-\(t\) polynomial \(g_i(x)\) with constant term \(\beta_i\). For honest parties \(P_j\), \(g_j(0)=\beta_j=f(\alpha_j)\). The vector \(\vec\gamma=(g_1(0),\ldots,g_n(0))\) differs in at most \(t\) positions from the Reed–Solomon codeword \((f(\alpha_1),\ldots,f(\alpha_n))\). Parties invoke \(F^H_{\mathrm{mat}}\), obtaining evaluations and syndrome \(\vec\gamma H^T\), then decode the error vector \(\vec e=\vec\gamma-(f(\alpha_1),\ldots,f(\alpha_n))\). If \(e_i=0\), they retain the received evaluations of \(g_i\). If \(e_i\ne0\), they reconstruct \(g_i\) and use \(g_i(0)-e_i=f(\alpha_i)\) as the corrected share.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p055-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=55; block=4; confidence=0.98 -->

## Protocol 6.8: Secure computation of \(F_{\mathrm{subshare}}^{\mathrm{VSS}}\) in the \(F_{\mathrm{mat}}^H\)-hybrid model

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p056-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=56; block=2; confidence=0.97 -->
Each \(P_i\) holds \(\beta_i\); the honest parties’ points \((\alpha_j,\beta_j)\) lie on one degree-\(t\) polynomial. Common input is a field \(F\) and distinct nonzero \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n\), determining the \(2t\times n\) Reed–Solomon parity-check matrix \(H\). On invocation, the corruption-aware matrix functionality receives the corrupted set \(I\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p056-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=56; block=3; confidence=0.98 -->
**Protocol 6.8.** Each party \(P_i\) chooses a random degree-\(t\) polynomial \(g_i(x)\) with \(g_i(0)=\beta_i\). The parties invoke \(F^H_{\mathrm{mat}}\), receiving \(g_1(\alpha_i),\ldots,g_n(\alpha_i)\) and syndrome \(\vec s=(g_1(0),\ldots,g_n(0))H^T\). Each party decodes \(\vec s\) to obtain \(\vec e=(e_1,\ldots,e_n)\). For every \(k\) with \(e_k=0\), it sets \(g'_k(\alpha_i)=g_k(\alpha_i)\). For every \(k\) with \(e_k\ne0\), parties exchange evaluations of \(g_k\), replace missing values by zero, and Reed–Solomon decode to reconstruct \(g_k(x)\); they set \(g'_k(\alpha_i)=g_k(0)-e_k\). Each party outputs \(g_i(x)\) and \(g'_1(\alpha_i),\ldots,g'_n(\alpha_i)\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p064-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=64; block=3; confidence=0.98 -->
Section 6.6 introduces a functionality that, given degree-t sharings A and B, produces a degree-t sharing C with C(0)=A(0)·B(0).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p064-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=64; block=4; confidence=0.95 -->
The functionality is used in the multiplication protocol described in Section 6.1.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p065-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=65; block=1; confidence=0.97 -->
**Functionality 6.13 (\(F^{\mathrm{mult}}_{VSS}\)).** Given corrupted set \(I\subseteq[n]\), the functionality receives \((a_j,b_j)\) from every honest party \(P_j\), \(j\notin I\). It determines the degree-\(t\) polynomials \(A,B\) interpolating these points. If the dealer \(P_1\) is honest, it chooses a random degree-\(t\) polynomial \(C\) with \(C(0)=A(0)B(0)\), sends \(C(x)\) to the dealer and \(C(\alpha_j)\) to each honest party, and sends \((A(\alpha_i),B(\alpha_i),C(\alpha_i))\) to the adversary for each \(i\in I\). If the dealer is corrupted, it sends \((A(x),B(x))\) to the adversary and receives \(C(x)\). If \(\deg(C)>t\) or \(C(0)\ne A(0)B(0)\), it resets \(C\) to the constant polynomial \(A(0)B(0)\), then sends \(C(\alpha_j)\) to every honest party. There is no further adversary output in this case.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p065-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=65; block=2; confidence=0.95 -->
The functionality exposes corrupted parties’ correct shares to help the simulator; in the intended multiplication use, the adversary already knows them.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p065-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=65; block=3; confidence=0.95 -->
Starting from shares of a and b represented by A(0)=a and B(0)=b, the goal is to share ab through C with C(0)=A(0)B(0).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p065-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=65; block=4; confidence=0.97 -->
The dealer chooses degree-t polynomials D₁,…,Dₜ so that C(x)=A(x)B(x)−∑_{ℓ=1}^t x^ℓDℓ(x) is random of degree at most t with constant term ab. Since A·B has degree at most 2t, the Dℓ terms are intended to cancel coefficients above degree t.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p066-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=66; block=1; confidence=0.98 -->
After sharing each Dℓ with F_VSS, party Pⱼ computes C(αⱼ)=A(αⱼ)B(αⱼ)−∑_{ℓ=1}^t(αⱼ)^ℓDℓ(αⱼ). Multiplication by positive powers of x preserves C(0)=A(0)B(0), even if the dealer chooses Dℓ maliciously. Honest dealing should yield a random degree-t sharing; malicious dealing still gives the correct constant term, but degree t must be checked.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p066-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=66; block=2; confidence=0.95 -->
A proposed check compares each locally computed C′(αⱼ) with a separately VSS-shared C(αⱼ), with complaints and reconstruction of A,B if more than t complaints occur. This does not establish the needed degree and constant-term conditions: a malicious dealer can choose Dℓ so the resulting higher-degree C agrees with another degree-t polynomial C′ at as many as 2t honest points while C′(0)≠ab. At least one of the ≥2t+1 honest parties detects a mismatch, but up to t false complaints can prevent acting on it when the dealer is honest.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p066-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=66; block=3; confidence=0.96 -->
A suggested alternative applies Protocol 5.6’s VSS verification to the locally computed shares C(αⱼ). If C is not degree t, F_VSS may alter the received points to form a degree-t polynomial, and that alteration can change the constant term, losing the guarantee C(0)=A(0)B(0).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p067-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=67; block=1; confidence=0.96 -->
Legitimate complaints trigger reconstruction of the input shares and output of the constant share \(a\cdot b\); illegitimate complaints are ignored. With no legitimate complaint, the shared degree-\(t\) polynomial \(C'\) agrees with \(C\), of degree at most \(2t\), at at least \(2t+1\) points, hence \(C=C'\) and has degree at most \(t\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p067-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=67; block=2; confidence=0.97 -->
For a complaint by \(P_k\), use \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{eval}}^k\) to reconstruct \(A(\alpha_k), B(\alpha_k), D_1(\alpha_k),\ldots,D_t(\alpha_k), C'(\alpha_k)\). Check whether \(C'(\alpha_k)=A(\alpha_k)B(\alpha_k)-\sum_{\ell=1}^t(\alpha_k)^\ell D_\ell(\alpha_k)\), equivalently whether \(C'(\alpha_k)=C(\alpha_k)\). Equality makes the complaint false; otherwise it is valid and the parties reconstruct \(a\cdot b\). The shares are available by the sharing and input assumptions. If the dealer is honest, no party can complain legitimately; an illegitimate complaint from a corrupted party reveals only that party's already-known shares.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p067-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=67; block=3; confidence=0.97 -->
The dealer chooses \(D_1(x),\ldots,D_t(x)\) so that \(C(x)=A(x)B(x)-\sum_{\ell=1}^t x^\ell D_\ell(x)\) is uniform among degree-at-most-\(t\) polynomials with constant term \(a b\), where \(a=A(0)\) and \(b=B(0)\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p067-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=67; block=4; confidence=0.92 -->
Define \(D(x)=A(x)B(x)=ab+d_1x+\cdots+d_{2t}x^{2t}\). Choose random \(r_{i,j}\in\mathbb F\) for \(1\le i\le t\), \(0\le j<t\). The displayed examples define \(D_t,D_{t-1},D_{t-2},\ldots,D_1\) with random coefficients below degree \(t\) and degree-\(t\) coefficients formed from the corresponding \(d_{t+i}\) minus selected random terms; the general formula continues on page 68.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p068-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=68; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
For \(1\le\ell\le t\), define
\[
D_\ell(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{t-1}r_{\ell,m}x^m+\left(d_{t+\ell}-\sum_{m=\ell+1}^{t}r_{m,t+\ell-m}\right)x^t,
\qquad C(x)=D(x)-\sum_{\ell=1}^t x^\ell D_\ell(x).
\]

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p068-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=68; block=2; confidence=0.99 -->
Writing \(D_\ell(x)=r_{\ell,0}+r_{\ell,1}x+\cdots+r_{\ell,t-1}x^{t-1}+R_{\ell,t}x^t\), the degree-\(t\) coefficient is
\[
R_{\ell,t}=d_{t+\ell}-\sum_{m=\ell+1}^{t}r_{m,t+\ell-m}.\tag{6.4}
\]

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p068-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=68; block=3; confidence=0.99 -->
In \(\sum_{\ell=1}^t x^\ell D_\ell(x)\), the coefficient of \(x^{t+k}\), for \(1\le k\le t\), is
\[
R_{k,t}+r_{k+1,t-1}+r_{k+2,t-2}+\cdots+r_{t,k}=R_{k,t}+\sum_{m=k+1}^t r_{m,t+k-m}=d_{t+k},
\]
by (6.4). Thus the \(x^{t+k}\) coefficient of \(C(x)\) is zero. Its constant term is \(ab\), since every subtracted term has a factor \(x^\ell\), \(\ell\ge1\). The coefficients of \(x,x^2,\ldots,x^t\) are random: each \(r_{i,0}\) occurs only in the coefficient of \(x^i\). Therefore \(C\) has degree at most \(t\), constant term \(ab\), and random remaining coefficients.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p068-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=68; block=4; confidence=0.97 -->
The protocol is specified in the \((\mathcal F_{\mathrm{VSS}},\mathcal F_{\mathrm{eval}}^1,\ldots,\mathcal F_{\mathrm{eval}}^n)\)-hybrid model; “\(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{eval}}\)-hybrid model” abbreviates this collection of evaluation functionalities.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p069-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=69; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
**Protocol 6.14.** Securely compute \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}}^{\mathrm{VSS}}\) in the \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{VSS}}\)-\(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{eval}}\)-hybrid model. The dealer \(P_1\) holds degree-\(t\) polynomials \(A,B\); each party \(P_i\) holds \(a_i=A(\alpha_i)\), \(b_i=B(\alpha_i)\). Common input: field \(\mathbb F\) and distinct nonzero \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n\in\mathbb F\). The VSS and evaluation functionalities are initialized with corrupted-party set \(I\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p069-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=69; block=2; confidence=0.99 -->
1. The dealer forms \(D(x)=A(x)B(x)=ab+\sum_{\ell=1}^{2t}d_\ell x^\ell\), samples the \(t^2\) values \(r_{k,j}\) independently and uniformly for \(1\le k\le t\), \(0\le j<t\), defines each \(D_\ell\) and \(C=D-\sum_{\ell=1}^t x^\ell D_\ell\) by the formulas on page 68, then shares \(C\) and each \(D_\ell\) using \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{VSS}}\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p069-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=69; block=3; confidence=0.99 -->
2. If any received share of \(C\) or \(D_\ell\) is \(\bot\), proceed to reject. Otherwise compute \(c'_i=a_ib_i-\sum_{\ell=1}^t(\alpha_i)^\ellD_\ell(\alpha_i)\). Broadcast \((\mathrm{complaint},i)\) if \(c'_i\ne C(\alpha_i)\); if no complaints occur, output \(C(\alpha_i)\), otherwise resolve complaints.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p069-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=69; block=4; confidence=0.99 -->
3. Initialize \(\mathrm{reject}=\mathrm{false}\). For each complaint by \(P_k\), run \(t+3\) evaluations \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{eval}}^k\) on the parties' \(a_i,b_i,C(\alpha_i),D_1(\alpha_i),\ldots,D_t(\alpha_i)\). Let the outputs be \(A(\alpha_k),B(\alpha_k),\widetilde C(\alpha_k),\widetilde D_1(\alpha_k),\ldots,\widetilde D_t(\alpha_k)\), and compute \(\widetilde C'(\alpha_k)=A(\alpha_k)B(\alpha_k)-\sum_{\ell=1}^t(\alpha_k)^\ell\widetilde D_\ell(\alpha_k)\). <a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p069-b005"></a>

**Theorem 6.15.** If $t<n/3$, Protocol 6.14 is $t$-secure in the $(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{VSS}},\mathcal F_{\mathrm{eval}})$-hybrid model with multiplication functionality $\mathcal F^{\mathrm{mult}}_{\mathrm{VSS}}$, against a static malicious adversary.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p070-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=70; block=2; confidence=0.96 -->
**Proof.** Consider separately an honest dealer and a corrupted dealer. For an honest dealer, the simulator obtains $(A(\alpha_i),B(\alpha_i),C(\alpha_i))$ for corrupted indices $i\in I$, samples $D_2,\ldots,D_t$ as random degree-$t$ polynomials, and sets
\[
D_1(\alpha_i)=\alpha_i^{-1}\!\left(A(\alpha_i)B(\alpha_i)-C(\alpha_i)-\sum_{\ell=2}^t\alpha_i^\ell D_\ell(\alpha_i)\right),\quad i\in I.
\]
This constraint follows from $C(x)=A(x)B(x)-\sum_{\ell=1}^t x^\ell D_\ell(x)$. The simulator then internally reproduces the VSS and evaluation interactions, resolves corrupted parties’ complaints using the known shares, and outputs the adversary’s output.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p071-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=71; block=1; confidence=0.98 -->
The simulator’s remaining steps deliver the computed shares through the simulated $\mathcal F_{\mathrm{VSS}}$ invocations, reproduce complaint-resolution evaluations when corrupted parties complain, and halt with the adversary’s output. The proof aims to establish equality of the ideal and hybrid execution distributions for every $I\subseteq[n]$, auxiliary input $z$, and input vector $\vec x$. It first compares honest-party outputs, then the adversary’s view conditioned on those outputs.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p071-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=71; block=2; confidence=0.98 -->
For honest input shares of degree-$t$ polynomials $A(x),B(x)$, the ideal functionality samples $C$ uniformly from $\mathcal P_{A(0)B(0),t}$. In the protocol, the dealer’s construction gives $\deg C\le t$ and $C(0)=A(0)B(0)$; with an honest dealer complaints are resolved without rejection. The nonconstant coefficients of $C$ are uniform, so the honest parties’ output shares have the same distribution as in the ideal execution.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p071-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=71; block=3; confidence=0.98 -->
Fix honest parties’ input and output shares. Since there are more than $t$ honest evaluation points, they determine $A,B,C$. The adversary’s view consists of its shares $\{D_1(\alpha_i)\}_{i\in I},\ldots,\{D_t(\alpha_i)\}_{i\in I},\{C(\alpha_i)\}_{i\in I}$ (6.5); complaint resolution reveals only a subset of these shares. It therefore suffices to compare the distributions of the $D_\ell(\alpha_i)$ values in ideal and protocol executions.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p072-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=72; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
Write $D^S_1,\ldots,D^S_t$ for simulator polynomials and $D_1,\ldots,D_t$ for dealer polynomials. The target equality is
\[
\{(D^S_1(\alpha_i),\ldots,D^S_t(\alpha_i))\mid A,B,C\}_{i\in I}\equiv\{(D_1(\alpha_i),\ldots,D_t(\alpha_i))\mid A,B,C\}_{i\in I}. \tag{6.6}
\]
It is reduced, for each $\ell=1,\ldots,t$, to equality of the conditional distributions of the $\ell$th shares given $A,B,C$ and all higher-index shares (6.7), combining indices from $t$ down to $1$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p072-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=72; block=2; confidence=0.94 -->
For $2\le\ell\le t$, simulator evaluations $D^S_\ell(\alpha_i)$ are uniform because $D^S_\ell$ is sampled uniformly. In the dealer construction, the coefficients of $x^0,\ldots,x^{t-1}$ in $D_\ell$ are independent uniform values, while dependence on higher-index polynomials is confined to its $x^t$ coefficient. Claim 3.5 implies that evaluations at any $t$ points remain uniform. The random values $r_{1,m-1}$ occur only in $D_1$ and make the nonconstant coefficients of $C$ uniform, yielding independence of $D_\ell$ from $C$ and proving (6.7) for $\ell>1$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p072-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=72; block=3; confidence=0.97 -->
For $\ell=1$, the dealer’s $D_1$ is determined by $C,D_2,\ldots,D_t$. Equivalently, sample $D_2,\ldots,D_t$ as before, sample $C$ uniformly from $\mathcal P_{A(0)B(0),t}$, and define
\[
D_1(x)=x^{-1}\left(A(x)B(x)-C(x)-\sum_{k=2}^t x^kD_k(x)\right). \tag{6.8}
\]
The page ends as this equivalent construction is introduced; the distributional comparison for this case is not completed in the supplied text.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p073-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=73; block=1; confidence=0.98 -->
**Proof step.** Conditioning on Eq. (6.7), the fixed polynomials determine the same values $D_1(\alpha_i)$ in the ideal and real executions. Thus the corrupted parties’ views have the same distribution given the honest outputs; since those outputs also have the same distribution, the joint distributions agree.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p073-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=73; block=2; confidence=0.98 -->
**Proof step.** If the dealer is corrupted, the ideal adversary submits $C(x)$ to $\mathcal F^{\mathrm{mult}}_{\mathrm{VSS}}$. When $\deg C\le t$ and $C(0)=A(0)B(0)$, honest parties’ outputs are determined by $C$; otherwise the functionality uses the constant polynomial $A(0)B(0)$. Deviations are detected by the $\mathcal F_{\mathrm{eval}}$ checks. The adversary can predict complaint locations and revealed values from $A,B,C,D_1,\ldots,D_t$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p073-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=73; block=3; confidence=0.98 -->

It rejects if any polynomial has degree greater than $t$. Otherwise, for each honest index $k\notin I$ violating
\[
C(\alpha_k)=A(\alpha_k)B(\alpha_k)-\sum_{\ell=1}^t(\alpha_k)^\ell D_\ell(\alpha_k),
\]
it simulates the complaint, then computes the complaint-resolution evaluations; any such honest complaint leads to rejection.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p074-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=74; block=1; confidence=0.98 -->
**Proof step.** For each adversarial complaint naming $k\in I$, the simulator computes the corresponding evaluation outputs and rejects if the same consistency equation fails at any corrupted index. If checks pass, it submits the adversary’s $C(x)$ to $\mathcal F^{\mathrm{mult}}_{\mathrm{VSS}}$. To simulate rejection it instead submits $\widehat C(x)=x^{t+1}$, then simulates honest parties broadcasting $a_j=A(\alpha_j)$ and $b_j=B(\alpha_j)$. Finally it returns the adversary’s output. Because it knows $A,B$, it can reproduce honest inputs and the adversary’s view.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p074-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=74; block=2; confidence=0.98 -->
**Proof step.** If the simulator does not reject, then all proposed polynomials have degree at most $t$, the consistency equation holds at every honest index, and it holds at each corrupted index that complained. In the real protocol honest parties output $C(\alpha_j)$. Let
\[
C'(x)=A(x)B(x)-\sum_{\ell=1}^t x^\ell D_\ell(x).
\]
Since each $D_\ell$ has degree at most $t$, $\deg C'\le 2t$. The agreement $C(\alpha_j)=C'(\alpha_j)$ at at least $2t+1$ honest points implies $C=C'$, hence $C(0)=C'(0)=A(0)B(0)$. Thus the ideal functionality also outputs $C(\alpha_j)$ to each honest party.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p074-b003"></a>

**Proof step.** If any acceptance condition fails, the real execution enters the reject phase and honest parties output $A(0)B(0)$. The simulator submits $x^{t+1}$, whose degree exceeds $t$, causing the ideal functionality to choose the constant polynomial $A(0)B(0)$ and produce the same outputs. This completes the proof.

## 6.7 The $\mathcal F^{\mathrm{mult}}$ Functionality and its Implementation

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p075-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=75; block=2; confidence=0.97 -->
The maliciously secure multiplication protocol shares subshares of each party’s two inputs, shares their products, then locally combines those shares by a linear map to obtain shares of the product of the original values.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p075-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=75; block=3; confidence=0.98 -->
**Definition (Functionality 6.16, $\mathcal F^{\mathrm{mult}}$).** Given corrupted-index set $I\subseteq[n]$, the functionality operates as follows:

1. Receive honest inputs $\{(\beta_j,\gamma_j)\}_{j\notin I}$. Let $f_a,f_b$ be the unique degree-at-most-$t$ polynomials interpolating $(\alpha_j,\beta_j)$ and $(\alpha_j,\gamma_j)$, respectively. If either does not exist, security is unspecified.
2. Send $\{(f_a(\alpha_i),f_b(\alpha_i))\}_{i\in I}$ to the ideal adversary.
3. Receive $\{\delta_i\}_{i\in I}$, defaulting any missing $\delta_i$ to $0$.
4. Choose a random degree-at-most-$t$ polynomial $f_{ab}$ satisfying
\[
f_{ab}(0)=f_a(0)f_b(0),\qquad f_{ab}(\alpha_i)=\delta_i\quad(i\in I).
\]
Such a polynomial exists because $|I|\le t$.
5. Send $f_{ab}(\alpha_j)$ to each honest party $j\notin I$.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p076-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=76; block=1; confidence=0.98 -->
Before proceeding, the text notes that \(F_{\mathrm{mult}}\) suffices for circuit emulation: although the adversary can determine \(t\) values of \(f_{ab}\), this reveals nothing about \(f_{ab}(0)=a\cdot b\). A formal proof is given in Section 7.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p076-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=76; block=2; confidence=0.97 -->
### Protocol idea

To multiply shared values, first use \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{subshare}}^{\mathrm{VSS}}\) to distribute subshares of the input shares, then use \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}}^{\mathrm{VSS}}\) to share products of those subshares.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p076-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=76; block=3; confidence=0.99 -->
Let \(f_a(0)=a\) and \(f_b(0)=b\), and write \(h(x)=f_a(x)f_b(x)=ab+h_1x+\cdots+h_{2t}x^{2t}\). With \(V_{\vec\alpha}\) the Vandermonde matrix, the coefficient vector \((ab,h_1,\ldots,h_{2t},0,\ldots,0)^T\) equals \(V_{\vec\alpha}^{-1}(h(\alpha_1),\ldots,h(\alpha_n))^T\). If \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n\) are the entries of the first row of \(V_{\vec\alpha}^{-1}\), then \(ab=\sum_{\ell=1}^n\lambda_\ell h(\alpha_\ell)=\sum_{\ell=1}^n\lambda_\ell f_a(\alpha_\ell)f_b(\alpha_\ell)\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p076-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=76; block=4; confidence=0.98 -->
For each \(\ell\), share a random degree-\(t\) polynomial \(C_\ell\) with constant \(C_\ell(0)=f_a(\alpha_\ell)f_b(\alpha_\ell)\), using party \(P_\ell\) as dealer. Each party locally forms \(Q(\alpha_i)=\sum_{\ell=1}^n\lambda_\ell C_\ell(\alpha_i)\), corresponding to \(Q(x)=\sum_{\ell=1}^n\lambda_\ell C_\ell(x)\). Thus
\[
Q(0)=\sum_{\ell=1}^n\lambda_\ell C_\ell(0)=\sum_{\ell=1}^n\lambda_\ell f_a(\alpha_\ell)f_b(\alpha_\ell)=ab. \tag{6.9}
\]
Since each \(C_\ell\) has degree \(t\), \(Q\) also has degree at most \(t\), giving a valid sharing of \(ab\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p077-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=77; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
**Protocol 6.17 (Computing \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}}\) in the \((\mathcal F_{\mathrm{subshare}}^{\mathrm{VSS}},\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}}^{\mathrm{VSS}})\)-hybrid model).**

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p077-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=77; block=2; confidence=0.98 -->
Each party \(P_i\) holds \(a_i=f_a(\alpha_i)\) and \(b_i=f_b(\alpha_i)\), where degree-\(t\) polynomials hide \(a,b\); inconsistent inputs receive no security guarantee. Common inputs are a field \(\mathbb F\) and distinct nonzero \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n\). The ideal functionalities are initialized with the corrupted-party set \(I\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p077-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=77; block=3; confidence=0.97 -->
1. Invoke \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{subshare}}^{\mathrm{VSS}}\) on each \(a_i\), yielding shares \(A_1(\alpha_i),\ldots,A_n(\alpha_i)\) and degree-\(t\) polynomial \(A_i\) with \(A_i(0)=a_i\).
2. Repeat on each \(b_i\), yielding \(B_j(\alpha_i)\) and degree-\(t\) polynomial \(B_i\) with \(B_i(0)=b_i\).
3. For each \(i\), let \(P_i\) deal in an invocation of \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}}^{\mathrm{VSS}}\), with all \(P_j\) supplying \(A_i(\alpha_j),B_i(\alpha_j)\). The dealer receives \(C_i(x)\in_R\mathcal P_{A_i(0)B_i(0),t}\), and each \(P_j\) receives \(C_i(\alpha_j)\).
4. Each \(P_i\) outputs \(Q(\alpha_i)=\sum_{\ell=1}^n\lambda_\ell C_\ell(\alpha_i)\), where \((\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)\) is the first row of \(V_{\vec\alpha}^{-1}\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p077-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=77; block=4; confidence=0.98 -->
Correctness follows from the preceding reconstruction identity. For security, the simulator must also generate the adversary’s parity-check polynomial vectors \(\vec Y_A(x)=(A_1(x),\ldots,A_n(x))H^T\) and \(\vec Y_B(x)=(B_1(x),\ldots,B_n(x))H^T\), as in the simulation for \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{eval}}\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p077-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=77; block=5; confidence=0.99 -->
**Theorem 6.18.** If \(t<n/3\), Protocol 6.17 is \(t\)-secure for \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}}\) in the \((\mathcal F_{\mathrm{subshare}}^{\mathrm{VSS}},\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}}^{\mathrm{VSS}})\)-hybrid model against a static malicious adversary.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p077-b006"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=77; block=6; confidence=0.97 -->
**Proof.** Assume honest inputs lie on degree-\(t\) polynomials; otherwise security is vacuous as noted in Footnote 11. The simulator interacts with the ideal \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}}\), internally runs the adversary, and simulates the honest parties and the two VSS functionalities.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p078-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=78; block=1; confidence=0.98 -->
The simulator \(S\) invokes adversary \(A\) with auxiliary input \(z\), then receives \((f_a(\alpha_i),f_b(\alpha_i))\) for every corrupted index \(i\in I\) from the ideal multiplication party.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p078-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=78; block=2; confidence=0.99 -->
For every \(j\notin I\), \(S\) chooses \(A_j(x)\in_R\mathcal P^{0,t}\), and internally gives \(A\) the values \(\{A_j(\alpha_i)\}_{j\notin I,\,i\in I}\). It receives the polynomials \(A_i(x)\) for \(i\in I\) from \(A\). For each \(i\in I\), it checks whether \(A_i(0)=f_a(\alpha_i)\) and whether \(\deg(A_i(x))=t\). If both checks pass, it sets \(A'_i(x)=A_i(x)\); otherwise, it sets \(A'_i(x)\) to the constant polynomial \(f_a(\alpha_i)\). For each \(j\notin I\), it sets \(A'_j(x)=A_j(x)\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p078-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=78; block=3; confidence=0.99 -->
The simulator sets \(e^A_j=0\) for \(j\notin I\), and \(e^A_i=A_i(0)-f_a(\alpha_i)\) for \(i\in I\). It chooses random polynomial vector \(\vec Y_A(x)=(Y_1(x),\ldots,Y_n(x))\) subject to \(\vec Y_A(0)=(e^A_1,\ldots,e^A_n)H^T\) and \(\vec Y_A(\alpha_i)=(A_1(\alpha_i),\ldots,A_n(\alpha_i))H^T\) for every \(i\in I\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p078-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=78; block=4; confidence=0.95 -->
Simulate the \(b_i\) subsharing identically, using \(f_b(\alpha_i)\) for corrupted indices; denote the resulting polynomials by \(B_j,B'_j\) and the parity-check vector by \(\vec Y_B\). At this point the simulator has degree-\(t\) polynomials \(A'_\ell,B'_\ell\), with \(A'_j(0)=B'_j(0)=0\) for honest \(j\notin I\), and \(A'_i(0)=f_a(\alpha_i),\ B'_i(0)=f_b(\alpha_i)\) for corrupted \(i\in I\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p079-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=79; block=1; confidence=0.98 -->
For each honest party index \(j\notin I\), simulator \(S\) simulates its \(\mathcal F^{\mathrm{mult}}_{\mathrm{VSS}}\) invocation by choosing \(C'_j\in_R P_{0,t}\) and giving the adversary the shares \(\{(A'_j(\alpha_i),B'_j(\alpha_i),C'_j(\alpha_i))\}_{i\in I}\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p079-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=79; block=2; confidence=0.98 -->
For each \(i\in I\), \(S\) simulates the corrupted dealer's invocation: it provides \((A'_i(x),B'_i(x))\), then receives the adversary's input polynomial \(C_i(x)\). If \(\deg C_i\le t\) and \(C_i(0)=A'_i(0)B'_i(0)=f_a(\alpha_i)f_b(\alpha_i)\), set \(C'_i=C_i\); otherwise set \(C'_i\) to the constant polynomial \(f_a(\alpha_i)f_b(\alpha_i)\). Consequently \(C'_j(0)=0\) for \(j\notin I\) and \(C'_i(0)=f_a(\alpha_i)f_b(\alpha_i)\) for \(i\in I\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p079-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=79; block=3; confidence=0.97 -->
For each \(i\in I\), \(S\) sends \(\delta_i=Q(\alpha_i)=\sum_{\ell=1}^n\lambda_\ell C'_{\ell}(\alpha_i)\) to \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}}\), then returns the adversary's output. The simulation differs from a real execution in the honest parties' constant terms, how \(\vec Y_A,\vec Y_B\) are computed, and how the output polynomial is sampled; the VSS checks themselves are simulated exactly. The proof introduces intermediate simulators bridging ideal and real executions.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p079-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=79; block=4; confidence=0.98 -->
**Definition.** \(S_1\) is \(S\) with additional input \(f_a(\alpha_j),f_b(\alpha_j)\) for every \(j\notin I\). For such \(j\), it chooses \(A'_j\in_R P_{f_a(\alpha_j),t}\), \(B'_j\in_R P_{f_b(\alpha_j),t}\), and \(C'_j\in_R P_{f_a(\alpha_j)f_b(\alpha_j),t}\). It still interacts with the ideal functionality \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}}\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p080-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=80; block=1; confidence=0.98 -->
**Claim.** The joint output distributions of the adversary and honest parties are equal for \(\mathrm{IDEAL}_{\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}},S}(z),I(\vec x)\) and \(\mathrm{IDEAL}_{\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}},S_1}(z'),I(\vec x)\), where \(z'\) augments \(z\) with \(f_a(\alpha_j),f_b(\alpha_j)\) for all \(j\notin I\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p080-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=80; block=2; confidence=0.97 -->
**Proof.** A machine using only the share points \(\{A'_j(\alpha_i),B'_j(\alpha_i),C'_j(\alpha_i)\}_{i\in I,j\notin I}\) can run the simulation. The same machine realizes the execution with \(S\) when the honest dealers' polynomial constant terms are zero, and the execution with \(S_1\) when they are \(f_a(\alpha_j),f_b(\alpha_j),f_a(\alpha_j)f_b(\alpha_j)\). Claim 3.4 gives identical distributions for the relevant share points under these two choices, so the joint outputs agree.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p080-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=80; block=3; confidence=0.98 -->
**Definition.** \(S_2\) is \(S_1\), except it computes \(\vec Y_A(x),\vec Y_B(x)\) from the actual polynomials \(A_1(x),\ldots,A_n(x)\), \(B_1(x),\ldots,B_n(x)\) that it chose, rather than from the error vectors.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p080-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=80; block=4; confidence=0.95 -->
The joint output distributions in \(\mathrm{IDEAL}_{\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}},S_2}(z'),I(\vec x)\) and \(\mathrm{IDEAL}_{\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}},S_1}(z'),I(\vec x)\) are equal, by the same argument used for \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{eval}}\), relying on the special property of the Syndrome function.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p080-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=80; block=5; confidence=0.98 -->
The joint outputs in the \((\mathcal F^{\mathrm{subshare}}_{\mathrm{VSS}},\mathcal F^{\mathrm{mult}}_{\mathrm{VSS}})\)-hybrid execution of protocol \(\pi\) with adversary \(A\), and in \(\mathrm{IDEAL}_{\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}},S_2}(z'),I(\vec x)\), are equal.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p081-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=81; block=1; confidence=0.94 -->
**Proof.** The output polynomial is the only remaining difference: the ideal functionality samples degree-\(t\) \(f_{ab}\) subject to \(f_{ab}(0)=f_a(0)f_b(0)\) and \(f_{ab}(\alpha_i)=\delta_i=Q(\alpha_i)\) for \(i\in I\), whereas the real protocol sets \(f_{ab}=Q=\sum_{\ell=1}^n\lambda_\ell C'_{\ell}\). The \(C'_\ell\) have the same distribution in both executions and correct constant terms. If \(|I|=t\), the \(t+1\) constraints determine the degree-\(t\) polynomial uniquely. If \(|I|<t\), the ideal can choose \(t-|I|\) independent uniform values \(\beta_\ell\in_R F\) at points \(\alpha_\ell\) with \(\ell\notin I\), then interpolate. In the real execution, Corollary 3.3 implies that for any such set of \(t-|I|\) points, the corresponding values \(f_{ab}(\alpha_\ell)\) are uniform, since the adversary sees none of the points \(C'_j(\alpha_\ell)\) at those indices. Thus both executions induce the same distribution of \(f_{ab}\), proving equality.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p081-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=81; block=2; confidence=0.97 -->
The corollary combines Theorems 5.7, 6.6, 6.9, 6.12, 6.15, and 6.18 by modular sequential composition.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p081-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=81; block=3; confidence=0.99 -->
**Corollary 6.19.** If \(t<n/3\), there is a protocol that securely computes \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}}\) in the plain model with private channels against a static malicious adversary corrupting at most \(t\) parties.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p082-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=82; block=1; confidence=0.95 -->
With bivariate VSS, parties already hold shares of other parties’ shares, so Protocol 6.17 can use \(\mathcal F^{\mathrm{mult}}_{\mathrm{VSS}}\) directly, without first invoking \(\mathcal F^{\mathrm{subshare}}_{\mathrm{VSS}}\). Applying the linear computation of \(Q(x)\) to the bivariate shares also lets the protocol output shares of the other parties’ shares for reuse in later multiplications. In the stated case, the total cost is \(n(t+1)\) invocations of \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{VSS}}\). Other protocols trade constant rounds for lower communication, and BGW gives a more efficient constant-round protocol when \(t<n/4\).

## 7 Secure Computation in the \((\mathcal F_{\mathrm{VSS}},\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}})\)-Hybrid Model

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p082-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=82; block=3; confidence=0.99 -->
### 7.1 Securely Computing Any Functionality

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p082-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=82; block=4; confidence=0.96 -->
The section constructs maliciously secure computation for any \(f\) with \(t<n/3\), assuming inputs lie in a known field \(F\), \(|F|>n\), and the parties share an arithmetic circuit \(C\) computing \(f:F^n\to F^n\). Each party’s input and output is one field element. The protocol uses \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{VSS}}\) for input sharing and the corruption-aware \(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}}\) for multiplication gates; correctness maintains valid wire shares, and Reed–Solomon decoding handles incorrect shares during output reconstruction.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p083-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=83; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
Since more than two-thirds of shares are correct, each recipient can use Reed–Solomon decoding to recover the correct output despite incorrect shares sent by corrupt parties.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p083-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=83; block=2; confidence=0.98 -->
**Protocol 7.1.** Each party \(P_i\) has input \(x_i\in\mathbb F\). The common input is a circuit \(C\) over \(\mathbb F\), with \(|\mathbb F|>n\), computing \(f:\mathbb F^n\to\mathbb F^n\), and distinct nonzero \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n\in\mathbb F\). The ideal functionalities receive the corrupted set \(I\).

1. **Input sharing:** Each \(P_i\) chooses uniformly a degree-\(t\) polynomial \(q_i(x)\) with constant term \(x_i\), then invokes \(F_{VSS}\) as dealer. Each party records the received shares \(q_1(\alpha_i),\ldots,q_n(\alpha_i)\), replacing \(\bot\) by zero.
2. **Circuit emulation:** Process gates in topological order. For an addition gate, set \(\delta_i^k=\beta_i^k+\gamma_i^k\). For multiplication by a constant \(c\), set \(\delta_i^k=c\beta_i^k\). For a multiplication gate, send \((\beta_i^k,\gamma_i^k)\) to \(F_{\mathrm{mult}}\) and use the returned value \(\delta_i^k\).
3. **Output reconstruction:** Let \(o_1,\ldots,o_n\) be the output wires, with \(P_i\)’s output on wire \(o_i\). For each output wire, parties send their shares to the designated recipient. Party \(P_i\) Reed–Solomon decodes the received shares to obtain a codeword, reconstructs its polynomial \(g_i(x)\), and outputs \(g_i(0)\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p084-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=84; block=1; confidence=0.99 -->
The claim below applies to ordinary functionalities: a circuit receiving only parties’ inputs cannot in general compute a corruption-aware functionality that also depends on the corrupted identities.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p084-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=84; block=2; confidence=0.99 -->
**Theorem 7.2.** For any \(f:F^n\to F^n\) and \(t<n/3\), Protocol 7.1, with common auxiliary input \(C\), is \(t\)-secure for \(f\) in the \((\mathcal F_{\mathrm{VSS}},\mathcal F_{\mathrm{mult}})\)-hybrid model against a static malicious adversary.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p084-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=84; block=3; confidence=0.97 -->
**Proof.** The proof argues that corrupted parties can only choose their own inputs: multiplication reveals no new information to the adversary and cannot affect honest parties’ outputs, while Reed–Solomon decoding protects output reconstruction. The simulator runs the adversary internally, simulates honest parties and the ideal calls, and interacts with the trusted party computing \(f\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p084-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=84; block=4; confidence=0.91 -->
The simulator invokes \(A\) with auxiliary input \(z\) and chooses \(\widehat I\supseteq I\) with \(|\widehat I|=t\). It simulates input sharing: for each honest party it chooses a random degree-\(t\) polynomial with constant term zero; for each corrupted party it obtains the polynomial selected by \(A\). The page specifies the shares sent to corrupted parties, the simulation of the VSS responses for corrupted dealers, and the stored share \(q_j(\alpha_i)\) for each corrupted party’s input wire. The exact scope of the indices in the first sampling clauses is unclear in the displayed text.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p084-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=84; block=5; confidence=0.98 -->
The simulator submits \(x_i=q_i(0)\) for each corrupt party \(i\in I\) to the trusted party computing \(f\), then receives the corresponding outputs \(y_i\), \(i\in I\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p084-b006"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=84; block=6; confidence=0.94 -->
For gates in topological order, the simulator maintains shares for indices in \(\widehat I\). At an addition gate, it computes \(\delta_i^k=\beta_i^k+\gamma_i^k\) for each such index and stores the result. At a multiplication-by-constant gate with constant \(c\), it computes \(\delta_i^k=c\beta_i^k\) and stores it. The supplied page ends before the simulator’s multiplication-gate case.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p085-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=85; block=1; confidence=0.93 -->
For a multiplication gate, the simulator gives \(A\) the stored input shares for the corrupted parties as the message to \(F_{\mathrm{mult}}\), then receives the corrupted parties’ inputs to that functionality. Any missing value is set to zero. It stores the resulting output shares for corrupted parties and generates additional random shares for the parties in \(\widehat I\setminus I\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p085-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=85; block=2; confidence=0.97 -->
For each corrupted party \(P_i\), let \(o_i\) be its output wire and \(\{\beta^i_\ell\}_{\ell\in\widehat I}\) the shares stored for that wire. The simulator finds the unique degree-\(t\) polynomial \(q'_i(x)\) such that \(q'_i(\alpha_\ell)=\beta^i_\ell\) for every \(\ell\in\widehat I\) and \(q'_i(0)=y_i\), where \(y_i\) is the output received from the trusted party. It simulates each honest party \(P_j\) sending \(q'_i(\alpha_j)\) to \(P_i\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p085-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=85; block=3; confidence=0.95 -->
**Definition.** The fictitious simulator \(S'\) acts like \(S\), but receives all inputs \(\vec x\) and chooses each honest party’s input polynomial from \(P_{x_j,t}\), with the correct constant term instead of constant term zero.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p085-b004"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=85; block=4; confidence=0.90 -->
**Proof step.** Equation (7.1) asserts equality of the joint output distributions of the adversary and honest parties under \(\mathrm{IDEAL}_{f,S,I}(\vec x)\) and \(\mathrm{IDEAL}_{f,S',I}(\vec x)\), over all inputs and auxiliary strings. A single ideal-model machine can simulate either using externally supplied points \(q_j(\alpha_i)\). Claim 3.4 says those points are identically distributed whether sampled from \(P_{0,t}\) or \(P_{x_j,t}\), yielding (7.1).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p085-b005"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=85; block=5; confidence=0.98 -->
**Proof step.** The next claimed equality is between the joint-output distributions of the ideal execution with \(S'\) and the \((\mathcal F_{VSS},\mathcal F_{mult})\)-hybrid execution of \(\pi\) against \(A\).

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p086-b001"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=86; block=1; confidence=0.98 -->
The Reed–Solomon code has length \(n\) and dimension \(t+1\), so it corrects up to \(\frac{n-(t+1)}2\) errors. Under the stated assumption \(t<n/3\), this is at least \(t\). Thus corrupted parties’ output-stage messages do not affect honest parties’ outputs.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p086-b002"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=86; block=2; confidence=0.93 -->
**Proof step.** Conditioned on honest outputs \(\{y_j\}_{j\notin I}\), the adversary’s view under \(S'\) matches its real-protocol view through the output stage: input polynomials agree and computation reveals no new values beyond already-known multiplication inputs. For an output wire directly from multiplication, the functionality selects a random sharing polynomial subject to constant term \(y_i\), as does \(S'\); hence the output shares sent to the adversary have identical distributions.

<a id="pdf-27f2b3787521-p086-b003"></a>
<!-- pdf-source: page=86; block=3; confidence=0.96 -->
**Corollary 7.3.** For every functionality \(f:F^n\to F^n\) and \(t<n/3\), a protocol exists that is \(t\)-secure for \(f\) in the plain model with private channels against a static malicious adversary. Derived from Theorem 5.7, Corollary 6.19, Theorem 7.2, and modular sequential composition.
